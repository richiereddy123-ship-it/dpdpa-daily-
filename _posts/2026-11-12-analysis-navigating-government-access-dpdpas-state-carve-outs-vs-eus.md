As of November 2026, India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) is fully operational, establishing a robust framework for personal data protection. However, a critical area of divergence from global standards, particularly those championed by the European Union, lies in the provisions for government access to personal data. This comparative analysis focuses on the DPDPA's carve-outs for state instrumentalities and how they measure against the stringent requirements set by EU jurisprudence, notably the Schrems II judgment, for cross-border data transfers and adequacy decisions.

## The DPDPA's Broad Exemption for State Instrumentalities

The DPDPA, while introducing significant data principal rights and data fiduciary obligations, includes a notable exemption for government entities. Section 17(2) permits the Central Government to exempt any "instrumentality of the State" from the application of *any* provision of the DPDPA. This exemption can be invoked for reasons such as national security, foreign relations, public order, and preventing incitement to cognizable offences. The power to grant such exemptions rests with the executive, exercised through notification in the Official Gazette.

Crucially, Section 17(2) itself does not explicitly mandate specific safeguards like necessity, proportionality, or independent oversight as conditions for invoking the exemption. While the Indian Supreme Court's landmark Puttaswamy judgment affirmed privacy as a fundamental right, the DPDPA's text for state access does not codify these principles directly as explicit statutory requirements for the exemption. This contrasts with the detailed conditions and oversight mechanisms commonly found in privacy frameworks that withstand international scrutiny, particularly from the EU. The breadth of "any provision" suggests that even core data principal rights, such as the right to information or correction, could be overridden for exempted entities.

## EU's Stringent Standards: Necessity, Proportionality, and Redress

The European Union's approach to government access to personal data, particularly in the context of international transfers, is profoundly shaped by the Court of Justice of the European Union (CJEU) in cases like Schrems II. This judgment invalidated the EU-US Privacy Shield, primarily due to concerns over US surveillance laws not providing an "essentially equivalent" level of protection to EU fundamental rights.

Under the EU framework, any state access to personal data must adhere to strict principles derived from the EU Charter of Fundamental Rights (Articles 7, 8, 47, and 52(1)). These principles dictate that surveillance measures must be:
1.  **Strictly Necessary:** The measure must address a genuine and pressing need.
2.  **Proportionate:** The interference with fundamental rights must be limited to what is strictly necessary to achieve the legitimate objective.
3.  **Subject to Independent Oversight:** There must be an effective mechanism for independent review of surveillance activities.
4.  **Provide Effective Judicial Redress:** Data subjects must have access to effective remedies before an independent and impartial tribunal to challenge such access.

These requirements are paramount when the European Commission assesses a third country's legal framework for an adequacy decision under GDPR Article 45. Furthermore, even when relying on alternative transfer mechanisms like Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs) under GDPR Article 46, data exporters are obliged to assess the third country's laws, including government access provisions, to ensure they do not undermine the protections offered by the SCCs.

## Divergent Approaches to Safeguards and Adequacy

The comparison reveals a significant divergence. The DPDPA's Section 17(2) provides a broad, executive-driven exemption for state instrumentalities, with the specific statutory text remaining silent on explicit requirements for necessity, proportionality, independent oversight, or specific redress mechanisms for data principals against state access. While the Indian constitutional framework provides fundamental rights, the DPDPA's specific carve-out does not embed these principles as direct conditions for the exemption.

In contrast, the EU framework demands these explicit safeguards as a prerequisite for data transfers. It scrutinizes the *entire* legal system of the third country, including its national security laws, to ensure an "essentially equivalent" level of protection. The absence of such explicit, statutory-mandated safeguards within the DPDPA's government exemption could pose a substantial challenge for India in its pursuit of an EU adequacy decision. The EU's bar for adequacy is not merely the existence of a data protection law, but the comprehensive protection of fundamental rights against all forms of interference, including state surveillance.

## Broader Indian Context: RBI and IT Rules

Beyond the DPDPA, other Indian regulatory frameworks also touch upon government access to data. The Reserve Bank of India's (RBI) directives, particularly data localisation mandates for payment system operators, require certain sensitive financial data to be stored exclusively in India. While these mandates are not directly about surveillance carve-outs, they ensure that a significant volume of personal data remains within India's borders, making it potentially more accessible to Indian state instrumentalities under the ambit of DPDPA Section 17(2).

Similarly, the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, specifically Rule 4(2), compel intermediaries to assist government agencies in accessing information for various purposes. These rules reinforce the state's power to access data, and any such access by an "instrumentality of the State" would likely fall under the DPDPA's Section 17(2) exemption, rather than providing additional safeguards for data principals. These complementary frameworks highlight a consistent theme of significant state access powers within the Indian legal landscape.

**Practical takeaway**
Indian businesses, particularly those engaged in cross-border data transfers with the EU or operating in sectors sensitive to government data requests, must meticulously assess the implications of DPDPA Section 17(2). For Data Protection Officers (DPOs) and General Counsels (GCs), this means conducting thorough Transfer Impact Assessments (TIAs) when relying on mechanisms like Standard Contractual Clauses for EU data transfers to India. Internal policies must clearly delineate procedures for responding to state access requests, acknowledging the broad scope of Section 17(2) and the absence of explicit statutory safeguards within it. Businesses should also closely monitor any judicial interpretations of DPDPA Section 17(2) and the broader constitutional challenges to the Act, as these will shape the practical landscape for data protection and state access in India.
