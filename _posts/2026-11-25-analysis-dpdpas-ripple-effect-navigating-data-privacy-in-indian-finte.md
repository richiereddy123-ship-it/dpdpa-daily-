The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA), now fully operational as of November 2026, has ushered in a new era of data governance in India. For the dynamic and data-intensive fintech sector, this marks a significant inflection point. Fintech companies, already navigating a complex web of regulations primarily from the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), must now seamlessly integrate the DPDPA's horizontal framework with existing sectoral mandates. The challenge lies not just in compliance with the DPDPA itself, but in harmonising its principles with RBI's stringent norms on data security, localisation, and consumer protection.

## Reimagining Consent and Legitimate Uses in Fintech

At the heart of the DPDPA lies the principle of consent. Section 6 mandates that personal data can only be processed for a lawful purpose for which the Data Principal has given their free, specific, informed, unconditional, and unambiguous consent. For fintechs, this means a rigorous overhaul of their customer onboarding and data processing practices. The days of broad, bundled consent are over; granular consent for each specific purpose, clearly communicated to the customer, is now the standard.

While the DPDPA introduces "deemed consent" under Section 7 for certain specified legitimate uses (such as providing a service explicitly requested by the Data Principal or for employment purposes), its applicability in the broader fintech landscape, especially for secondary data uses like cross-selling or behavioural analytics, remains limited. Fintechs often rely on extensive data analysis for fraud detection, credit scoring, and personalised product offerings. These activities must now be carefully assessed against the DPDPA's "reasonable purposes" under Section 7(e) and the overarching requirement for transparency. Unlike the broader "legitimate interest" basis seen in the EU's GDPR, DPDPA's deemed consent is more circumscribed, demanding a closer alignment with the Data Principal's reasonable expectations and the primary purpose of data collection. This necessitates a clear articulation of how such data processing serves the public interest or is essential for the provision of the core financial service. Furthermore, the treatment of legacy personal data, collected before the DPDPA's enactment, requires careful re-evaluation to ensure compliance with Section 5(2) and any specific transition rules issued by the government.

## Data Fiduciary Obligations and Principal Rights: A Dual Mandate

Fintech entities, as 'Data Fiduciaries' under Section 2(j) of the DPDPA, bear significant responsibilities. They are obligated to implement reasonable security safeguards to prevent data breaches (Section 9), a mandate that aligns closely with RBI's existing robust cybersecurity frameworks for regulated entities. The DPDPA further strengthens this by requiring notification to the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) and affected Data Principals in the event of a personal data breach (Section 17). This complements RBI's incident reporting guidelines, creating a dual reporting mechanism that demands swift and transparent action.

Moreover, the DPDPA empowers Data Principals with a suite of rights, including the right to access information about their data (Section 12), the right to correction and erasure (Section 13), and the right to grievance redressal (Section 14). These rights significantly enhance consumer protection in the financial sector. Fintechs must establish robust mechanisms for Data Principals to exercise these rights, integrating them with existing RBI-mandated customer service and grievance redressal frameworks. For significant Data Fiduciaries (SDFs), which many large fintechs will likely be designated as, the appointment of a Data Protection Officer (DPO) and independent data audits (Section 10) will be mandatory, adding another layer of accountability. The DPDPA's stance on cross-border data transfers (Section 16), permitting transfers to notified countries, will also need careful reconciliation with RBI's strict data localisation requirements, particularly for payment system data. Where RBI mandates stricter localisation, those norms will generally prevail, reinforcing India's sovereign control over critical financial data.

## Interplay with Sectoral Regulators: RBI's Evolving Role

The DPDPA, as stated in Section 41, operates in addition to and not in derogation of any other law. This means that fintechs must comply with both the DPDPA and all applicable RBI regulations. Where a provision of another law offers a higher degree of protection to the Data Principal, that provision will take precedence. This principle is crucial for the fintech sector, where RBI's norms often set high standards for data security and consumer protection.

The expectation is that RBI will issue further clarifications or updated guidelines to harmonise its existing directives – such as those on digital lending, payment aggregators, outsourcing of financial services, and IT governance – with the DPDPA. This will likely involve specific interpretations of DPDPA provisions within the financial context, potentially stipulating how consent should be obtained for various financial products, how data breaches should be reported, and the specific roles of DPOs in regulated entities. The Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) will serve as the primary enforcement authority for DPDPA violations, but its collaboration with RBI on matters concerning regulated financial entities will be essential to ensure consistent and effective oversight.

## Practical Takeaway

For Indian fintech businesses, General Counsels, and Data Protection Officers, the DPDPA signifies a maturity curve in data governance. It demands a proactive and integrated approach to privacy. Start by conducting a thorough data mapping exercise to understand what personal data is collected, where it resides, how it's processed, and for what purposes. Revisit all consent mechanisms to ensure DPDPA compliance, especially for existing customer data. Strengthen data security infrastructure and incident response plans, aligning them with both DPDPA breach notification requirements and RBI's cybersecurity mandates. Invest in robust DPO functions and privacy teams, ensuring they have the necessary expertise to navigate this dual regulatory landscape. Finally, stay attuned to forthcoming guidelines from both the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) and the RBI, as these will provide crucial clarity for sector-specific implementation. Compliance is no longer a check-box exercise but a continuous journey of building trust through responsible data practices.
