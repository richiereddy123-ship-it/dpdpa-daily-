The landscape of data protection has been fundamentally reshaped by the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) and its subsequent Rules, 2025, bringing India's regulatory framework for data breaches into sharper focus. For businesses operating in or with India, understanding these new domestic obligations alongside established global standards from the EU, Singapore, and Australia is paramount. This analysis compares the critical aspect of breach notification timelines, anchoring our perspective in India's evolving regime.

## India's Evolving Framework: DPDPA and Beyond

With the DPDP Rules 2025 now in effect, India has a comprehensive framework for personal data breach notifications. The DPDPA 2023, under Section 22(7), mandates that Data Fiduciaries must notify the Data Protection Board of India and affected Data Principals of a personal data breach in the prescribed form and manner. The DPDP Rules 2025 specify this further, requiring notification to the Data Protection Board within 72 hours of becoming aware of a personal data breach. For affected Data Principals, notification is required "without undue delay," particularly when the breach poses a significant risk of harm. This risk-based approach for individuals aligns with international best practices.

However, India's framework is layered. Beyond the DPDPA, existing sector-specific and cybersecurity regulations impose additional, often stricter, notification obligations. The Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In) Directions, 2022, for instance, mandate that all service providers, data centres, body corporates, and government organisations report cyber incidents to CERT-In within 6 hours of becoming aware. Similarly, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has its own stringent requirements for regulated entities, often demanding notification of specific cyber incidents within 6 hours to the RBI. This creates a complex compliance environment where the strictest applicable timeline must be adhered to.

## EU's Prescriptive Approach: GDPR Article 33

The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) sets a globally recognized benchmark for data breach notification. Under Article 33(1), a data controller must notify the competent supervisory authority of a personal data breach within 72 hours of becoming aware of it, unless the breach is unlikely to result in a risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons. This introduces a materiality threshold for regulator notification.

For affected data subjects, GDPR Article 34(1) requires notification "without undue delay" if the personal data breach is likely to result in a high risk to their rights and freedoms. This "high risk" threshold means not all breaches necessitate direct communication with individuals, allowing controllers to focus resources where the impact is most severe. The GDPR's clear timelines and risk-based assessment have influenced many subsequent privacy laws worldwide.

## Asia-Pacific's Flexible Frameworks: Singapore and Australia

In the Asia-Pacific region, Singapore and Australia offer frameworks with slightly different nuances, particularly concerning the timing and thresholds for notification. Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA) 2012, as amended in 2020, mandates notification under Section 26C. Data organisations must notify the Personal Data Protection Commission (PDPC) and affected individuals "as soon as practicable" if the breach is likely to result in significant harm to individuals or is of a significant scale (involving 500 or more individuals). The "as soon as practicable" standard offers more flexibility than a fixed hour count but still implies urgency. The dual threshold of "significant harm" or "significant scale" broadens the scope of mandatory notification to individuals.

Australia's Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme, under the Privacy Act 1988, Section 26WL, also employs the "as soon as practicable" standard for notifying the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) and affected individuals. This notification is triggered if a data breach is likely to result in "serious harm" to any individual whose personal information is involved. Like Singapore, Australia's framework prioritizes prompt action without imposing a rigid hourly deadline, while focusing on the potential for serious harm to individuals.

## Comparative Strictness and Nuances

Comparing these regimes reveals a spectrum of strictness and flexibility. India's DPDP Rules 2025, with a 72-hour timeline to the Data Protection Board and "without undue delay" for individuals (where significant risk exists), largely aligns with the GDPR's core principles. However, India stands out for the overlay of CERT-In and RBI guidelines, which often impose a significantly stricter 6-hour reporting window for specific cyber incidents, making India potentially the most demanding jurisdiction for certain types of breaches affecting regulated sectors or critical infrastructure.

In contrast, Singapore and Australia's "as soon as practicable" standard offers more operational flexibility than the fixed 72-hour deadlines of India (DPDP Rules) and the EU (GDPR). While this allows for more thorough initial assessment, it also places a greater burden on organisations to demonstrate reasonable promptness. The thresholds for notifying individuals also vary: India (DPDP Rules) and GDPR focus on "significant risk" or "high risk," while Singapore adds "significant scale," and Australia uses "serious harm." These nuanced differences require careful interpretation and robust internal assessment capabilities.

## Practical Takeaway

For Indian businesses, General Counsels, and Data Protection Officers, the primary takeaway is the imperative for a highly agile and multi-layered incident response plan. Given the overlapping and potentially stricter timelines imposed by CERT-In and RBI, simply complying with the 72-hour DPDP Rules may not suffice. Organisations must prioritize rapid detection, thorough initial assessment, and immediate notification to the relevant authorities based on the most stringent applicable timeline. A harmonized incident response strategy that can simultaneously address DPDPA, CERT-In, RBI, and international obligations will be crucial for navigating India's complex data protection landscape and maintaining global compliance.
