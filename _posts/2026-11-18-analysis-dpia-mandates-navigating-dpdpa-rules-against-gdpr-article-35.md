As of November 18, 2026, India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) is fully operational, with its accompanying Rules now in effect. A critical component of robust data governance, and a staple in global privacy frameworks, is the Data Protection Impact Assessment (DPIA). For Indian entities, understanding how the DPDPA Rules' approach to DPIAs compares with the well-established GDPR Article 35 is crucial for effective compliance in an increasingly interconnected regulatory landscape.

## The Foundational Mandates: DPDPA Rules vs. GDPR
The DPDPA itself lays a broad foundation, empowering the Central Government to prescribe specific measures. Section 10(1) mandates that a Data Fiduciary undertake "such other measures as may be prescribed" to ensure compliance. More specifically, Section 10(3) outlines additional obligations for entities designated as Significant Data Fiduciaries (SDFs), which are now detailed in the DPDPA Rules. These Rules are understood to prescribe a requirement for Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) or a similar risk assessment framework for certain processing activities. This approach means the DPDPA's direct legislative text defers the specifics of DPIAs entirely to the Rules, providing flexibility for the government to adapt requirements.

In contrast, GDPR Article 35 directly and explicitly mandates DPIAs for data controllers. Article 35(1) states that a DPIA is required "where a type of processing, in particular using new technologies, and taking into account the nature, scope, context and purposes of the processing, is likely to result in a high risk to the rights and freedoms of natural persons." This direct legislative prescription in the GDPR offers greater upfront clarity regarding the obligation itself, though the specifics of "high risk" are further elaborated by supervisory authority guidelines.

## Triggers and Scope: When is a DPIA Required?
Under the DPDPA Rules, the triggers for conducting a DPIA are primarily linked to the designation of an entity as a Significant Data Fiduciary (SDF) under Section 10(2), or specific types of processing activities that pose elevated risks. While the precise criteria are detailed in the Rules, it is anticipated that large-scale processing of sensitive personal data, extensive profiling, processing of biometric data, or any processing likely to have a significant impact on Data Principals' rights would necessitate a DPIA. This means that for non-SDFs, the requirement might be narrower or less explicitly defined, making the DPDPA regime potentially looser for a broader base of data fiduciaries compared to GDPR.

GDPR Article 35(1) applies to *any* controller undertaking processing "likely to result in a high risk." Article 35(3) provides non-exhaustive examples, such as systematic and extensive evaluation of personal aspects based on automated processing, large-scale processing of special categories of data or data relating to criminal convictions, and systematic monitoring of a publicly accessible area on a large scale. This "high risk" threshold is universally applied, making GDPR potentially stricter in its universal application across all controllers, irrespective of their size or designation, if their processing activities meet the high-risk criteria.

## Content and Consultation: What Must Be Assessed?
The DPDPA Rules are understood to require a DPIA to include a systematic description of the processing operations, an assessment of the necessity and proportionality of the processing, an assessment of the risks to Data Principals, and the measures envisaged to address those risks.
