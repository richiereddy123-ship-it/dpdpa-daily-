The rapid expansion of edtech platforms has transformed learning, but it has also brought the sensitive issue of student data privacy to the forefront. For Indian edtech companies operating domestically and internationally, understanding the nuances of various data protection regimes is crucial. As of November 22, 2026, India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA), anchors our approach, but global players must also contend with the Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act (FERPA) in the United States and the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in the European Union. This analysis compares these frameworks, highlighting their similarities, differences, and specific implications for student data.

## Scope and Definitions of Personal Data

India's DPDPA applies broadly to the processing of digital personal data within India and to processing outside India if it relates to offering goods or services to data principals in India (Section 1). "Personal data" is defined as any data about an individual who is identifiable by or in relation to such data (Section 2(9)). An edtech company would be a "data fiduciary," and a student or their parent/guardian would be a "data principal." The DPDPA also introduces the concept of a "significant data fiduciary" (SDF) based on factors like the volume and sensitivity of personal data processed, which many large edtech platforms are likely to become, incurring additional obligations (Section 10).

In contrast, FERPA (20 U.S.C. § 1232g) is a U.S. federal law that protects the privacy of "education records." It applies to educational agencies and institutions that receive funds under any program administered by the U.S. Department of Education. "Education records" are broadly defined as records directly related to a student and maintained by an educational agency or institution. FERPA primarily regulates these institutions, not directly the edtech vendors. Vendors become subject to FERPA through contractual agreements with schools.

The GDPR (Article 2) has a broad territorial scope, applying to controllers and processors established in the EU, or those offering goods/services to data subjects in the EU. "Personal data" (Article 4(1)) is defined as any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person, a definition largely echoed by the DPDPA. The GDPR also identifies "special categories of personal data" (Article 9), such as health data, which might be relevant if edtech platforms collect such sensitive information.

Compared to FERPA's institutional and record-specific focus, both DPDPA and GDPR offer a wider and more direct regulatory reach over edtech companies themselves, defining "personal data" more broadly than FERPA's "education records."

## Consent and Lawful Basis for Processing

The DPDPA places strong emphasis on "consent" as the primary lawful basis for processing personal data (Section 6). Consent must be free, specific, informed, unconditional, and unambiguous, indicated by an affirmative action. Critically, for children (individuals under 18 years of age), processing requires "verifiable parental consent" (Section 9(1)). The DPDPA also outlines "legitimate uses" where consent is not required (Section 7), but these are generally for specific public interest or legal purposes, less applicable to routine edtech operations.

FERPA generally requires written parental consent before an educational institution can disclose "personally identifiable information" (PII) from a student's education record (20 U.S.C. § 1232g(b)). There are exceptions, such as disclosure to school officials with legitimate educational interests or in response to a judicial order. Once a student turns 18 or attends a post-secondary institution, these rights transfer to the student ("eligible student").

The GDPR requires a "lawful basis" for processing personal data (Article 6). While consent (Article 6(1)(a)) is one such basis, others include performance of a contract (Article 6(1)(b)) or legitimate interests (Article 6(1)(f)). For "information society services" offered directly to a child, consent must be given or authorised by the holder of parental responsibility, typically for children under 16, though Member States can set this age lower (Article 8).

Indian law is stricter than FERPA by placing the direct burden of obtaining consent on the edtech company, not just the institution, and setting a higher age of parental consent at 18. Both DPDPA and GDPR require explicit, affirmative consent for children, but DPDPA's 18-year threshold is notably higher than GDPR's typical 13-16 range.

## Data Principal Rights and Fiduciary Obligations

Under the DPDPA, data principals have several rights, including the right to obtain information about their personal data (Section 11), the right to correction and erasure (Section 12), the right to grievance redressal (Section 13), and the unique "right to nominate" another person to exercise these rights in case of death or incapacity (Section 14). Data fiduciaries have obligations including ensuring accuracy and completeness of data (Section 8(1)), implementing reasonable security safeguards (Section 8(5)), and notifying the Data Protection Board of India and affected data principals of data breaches (Section 17).

FERPA grants parents and eligible students the right to inspect and review education records, seek to amend records they believe are inaccurate or misleading, and generally control the disclosure of PII from these records (20 U.S.C. § 1232g(a)). Educational institutions must provide annual notice of these rights.

The GDPR provides extensive data subject rights (Articles 12-22), including the right to be informed, right of access, right to rectification, right to erasure ("right to be forgotten"), right to restriction of processing, right to data portability, and the right to object to processing. Controllers and processors have obligations such as implementing data protection by design and by default (Article 25), maintaining records of processing activities (Article 30), conducting Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) for high-risk processing (Article 35), and appointing a Data Protection Officer (DPO) in certain cases (Article 37).

DPDPA and GDPR grant more comprehensive and direct rights to individuals against data handlers. FERPA's rights are primarily exercised against educational institutions. GDPR's requirements for DPIAs and DPOs are more explicit and detailed than DPDPA's current framing, although the DPDPA's SDF obligations may lead to similar practices.

## Data Security and Cross-Border Transfers

The DPDPA mandates that data fiduciaries implement "reasonable security safeguards" to prevent personal data breaches (Section 8(5)) and notify the Data Protection Board of India and affected data principals in such events (Section 17). For cross-border transfers, the DPDPA permits transfer of personal data to any country or territory outside India, unless the Central Government restricts such transfer to specific countries through notification (Section 16). This means transfers are generally allowed unless explicitly prohibited.

FERPA requires educational institutions to protect the privacy of PII in education records but does not prescribe specific technical security measures. Given its U.S.-centric scope, FERPA does not explicitly address cross-border data transfers.

The GDPR requires controllers and processors to implement "appropriate technical and organisational measures" to ensure a level of security appropriate to the risk (Article 32). It also has stringent rules for international transfers of personal data (Articles 44-50), permitting them only if the recipient country ensures an "adequate level of protection," or through specific safeguards like Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs) or Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs).

GDPR has the most prescriptive and stringent framework for cross-border data transfers, requiring specific mechanisms. The DPDPA permits transfers by default, with the government retaining the power to restrict. FERPA is largely silent on cross-border transfers, focusing on domestic institutional obligations. All three frameworks mandate robust security measures and breach notification, though with varying levels of detail.

## Practical takeaway

For Indian edtech businesses, GCs, and DPOs, a proactive and comprehensive privacy strategy is essential. Anchor your compliance efforts in the DPDPA, particularly its stringent requirements for verifiable parental consent for children under 18 (Section 9) and robust security safeguards (Section 8). Map your data flows to understand where student data originates, resides, and is transferred. When engaging with U.S. educational institutions, understand their FERPA obligations and ensure your contracts align with those requirements, treating the institution as the primary data controller. For operations involving EU students, align with GDPR's broader lawful bases, extensive data subject rights, and strict cross-border transfer mechanisms. While DPDPA shares many principles with GDPR, pay close attention to differences in the age of consent for children and the specifics of data transfer mechanisms. Implementing "privacy by design" and maintaining clear, transparent privacy notices will serve as a strong foundation for navigating this complex global landscape.
