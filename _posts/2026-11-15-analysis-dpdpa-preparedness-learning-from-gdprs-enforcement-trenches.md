As of November 2026, with the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) now firmly in force and the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules (DPDP Rules) providing further operational clarity, Indian businesses are navigating a new era of data governance. While the DPDPA is a distinctly Indian legislative framework, global privacy developments, particularly the enforcement trends under the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), offer invaluable foresight. Indian Data Fiduciaries can pre-empt common pitfalls and strengthen their compliance posture by understanding where their European counterparts faced scrutiny and penalties.

## Revamping Consent Mechanisms and Notices

One of the most significant areas of GDPR enforcement has been the stringent interpretation of valid consent. European regulators have repeatedly penalised organisations for obtaining consent that is not freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous. Broad "accept all" checkboxes, pre-ticked boxes, or bundled consents for multiple unrelated purposes have been deemed non-compliant.

The DPDPA echoes these principles. Section 7 mandates that consent must be accompanied by a notice detailing the personal data to be collected, the purpose of processing, and the Data Principal's rights. Crucially, Section 6 requires consent to be "specific" and indicated by a "clear and affirmative action." This moves Indian businesses far beyond the perfunctory "terms and conditions apply" approach. Data Fiduciaries must now ensure their consent notices are granular, transparent, and easily understandable, potentially in multiple Indian languages as per forthcoming DPDP Rules. They must also provide a clear mechanism for Data Principals to withdraw consent at any time (Section 6(4)), a right frequently exercised under GDPR. The challenge of managing consent for legacy data, collected before DPDPA's enactment, will require careful attention to ensure ongoing processing has a valid legal basis.

## Upholding Data Principal Rights and Grievance Redressal

GDPR enforcement has underscored the importance of robust mechanisms for Data Principals to exercise their rights, including the right to access, correction, erasure, and portability of their data. Failures to respond to these requests promptly or adequately have led to significant fines.

Under the DPDPA, Data Principals are granted similar, albeit distinct, rights. Section 13(1) outlines rights such as the right to information about personal data, correction and erasure, and grievance redressal. Section 14 mandates that every Data Fiduciary appoint a Grievance Redressal Officer (GRO) and establish an effective mechanism for Data Principals to register complaints. For Significant Data Fiduciaries (SDFs), Section 10(2) further requires the appointment of an independent Data Protection Officer and an independent data auditor. This structure, while not identical to GDPR's DPO, places a clear onus on Indian organisations to establish accessible, efficient, and transparent processes for handling data principal requests and complaints, mirroring the accountability seen in GDPR enforcement.

## Strengthening Data Security and Breach Notification

Data breaches have been a consistent source of GDPR fines, with penalties often stemming not just from the breach itself, but from inadequate security measures or delayed/insufficient notification to supervisory authorities and affected individuals. The 72-hour notification window under GDPR has been a particular challenge.

The DPDPA, under Section 10(1), obligates Data Fiduciaries to implement "reasonable security safeguards" to prevent personal data breaches. More critically, Section 11 mandates that in the event of a personal data breach, the Data Fiduciary must notify the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) and affected Data Principals in a manner and within timelines prescribed by the DPDP Rules. While specific timelines for India are yet to be fully detailed, the spirit of prompt and transparent notification is clear. Indian companies must therefore invest in robust cybersecurity frameworks, conduct regular security audits, and develop comprehensive incident response plans that integrate DPDPA's notification requirements with existing frameworks like CERT-In guidelines.

## Managing Data Processors and Third-Party Risks

GDPR enforcement has consistently held Data Controllers accountable for the actions of their Data Processors. Inadequate due diligence, insufficient contractual clauses, and a lack of oversight over vendors handling personal data have resulted in penalties.

The DPDPA explicitly places responsibility on the Data Fiduciary for the actions of its Data Processors (Section 8(4)). This means that while a Data Fiduciary may outsource processing activities, its ultimate accountability for protecting personal data remains. Indian businesses frequently rely on a vast ecosystem of third-party vendors, cloud providers, and service partners. Under DPDPA, it becomes imperative to review all contracts with Data Processors to ensure they adequately reflect DPDPA obligations, including security safeguards, audit rights, and breach notification protocols. Due diligence on vendor security postures and regular monitoring will be crucial to mitigate risks and avoid vicarious liability under the DPDPA.

## Practical takeaway

Indian businesses, General Counsels, and Data Protection Officers must view DPDPA compliance not as a static checklist, but as an ongoing commitment to data principal rights and robust data governance. Proactive steps include comprehensive data mapping to understand what data is collected and processed, overhauling consent mechanisms to align with DPDPA's granular requirements, establishing efficient grievance redressal channels, developing integrated data breach response plans, and rigorously reviewing all third-party vendor contracts. Learning from GDPR's enforcement history offers a clear roadmap to navigate the DPDPA landscape, ensuring compliance and building trust in India's digital economy.
