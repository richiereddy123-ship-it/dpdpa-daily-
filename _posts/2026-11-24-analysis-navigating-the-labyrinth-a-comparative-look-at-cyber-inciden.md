As India's digital economy continues its rapid expansion, the landscape of cybersecurity incident reporting has become increasingly complex, demanding meticulous attention from businesses operating within the country and those with global footprints. With the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) now in effect, alongside existing CERT-In directions and sector-specific regulations, Indian entities face a multi-layered compliance challenge. This analysis compares India's framework with the European Union's NIS2 Directive and the US SEC's cybersecurity rules, highlighting key distinctions for an India-first audience.

## India's Multi-layered Reporting Mandates

India’s approach to cybersecurity incident reporting is characterized by its breadth and stringency across various regulatory bodies. The primary directive stems from the Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In), operating under the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology. The CERT-In Directions, issued under Section 70B(6) of the Information Technology Act, 2000, mandate that "service providers, data centres, body corporate and government organisations" report a wide array of cyber security incidents within a tight timeframe of six hours of noticing or being brought to notice of such incidents. This list includes specific types like data breaches, unauthorized access, defacement, and malicious code attacks. The reporting authority is CERT-In itself.

Adding a crucial layer, the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) introduces obligations for "Data Fiduciaries" regarding personal data breaches. Section 17 of the DPDPA requires Data Fiduciaries to notify the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) and affected Data Principals of a personal data breach "in such manner as may be prescribed." While the specific rules detailing the exact timeline and content of these notifications are continually being refined, the statutory obligation to report to both the regulator and individuals is firm. This significantly broadens the scope of incidents requiring individual notification beyond CERT-In's remit.

For regulated financial entities, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) imposes additional, often stricter, reporting requirements. Various RBI circulars and master directions (e.g., the Master Direction on Cyber Security Framework in Banks) mandate reporting of cyber incidents to the RBI within very short timeframes, sometimes as little as two to six hours for critical incidents, depending on their nature and impact. This often runs parallel to, or even supersedes, CERT-In's general directions for these specific sectors.

## EU's NIS2: Enhancing Resilience Across Critical Sectors

In the European Union, the NIS2 Directive (Directive (EU) 2022/2555), which member states were required to transpose into national law by October 2024, significantly expands incident reporting obligations. NIS2 applies to "essential" and "important" entities across 18 critical sectors, ranging from energy and transport to digital infrastructure and healthcare. Article 23 of NIS2 mandates that these entities report incidents that have a "significant impact" on the provision of their services.

The reporting timeline under NIS2 is tiered: an initial notification must be made to the relevant national Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT) or competent authority within 24 hours of becoming aware of a significant incident. This is followed by an updated assessment within 72 hours, and a final report within one month. NIS2 also includes provisions for notifying recipients of services about incidents that may adversely affect them and, where there is a "significant impact on individuals," informing those individuals.

## US SEC Rules: Materiality for Market Transparency

Across the Atlantic, the US Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) adopted new rules in 2023 requiring publicly traded companies to disclose material cybersecurity incidents. Item 1.05 of Form 8-K mandates that companies disclose any "material cybersecurity incident" within four business days of determining that the incident is material. The focus here is on the incident's potential impact on the company's financial condition or operations, and by extension, its investors.

The SEC rules also require annual disclosure in Form 10-K regarding a company’s cybersecurity risk management strategy, governance, and management’s role in assessing and managing cybersecurity risks. Unlike the Indian or EU frameworks, the SEC rules do not directly mandate individual notification of data breaches; their focus is on investor protection and market transparency.

## Key Differences and Overlaps

Comparing these frameworks reveals several critical distinctions. India's CERT-In Directions are arguably the most stringent in terms of initial reporting timelines, demanding notification within six hours for a broad range of incidents, regardless of their immediate impact on personal data or financial materiality. This is significantly stricter than NIS2's 24-hour initial notification or the SEC's four-business-day window post-materiality determination.

The DPDPA, however, introduces a layer of individual notification for personal data breaches, aligning India with global data protection standards like NIS2 (which includes provisions for individual notification under certain circumstances) but contrasting with the SEC rules, which are silent on this aspect. Where an incident involves personal data, an Indian entity would likely face dual reporting obligations: to CERT-In for the cybersecurity incident itself and to the DPBI (and Data Principals) under the DPDPA for the personal data breach.

NIS2's scope is sector-specific, targeting "essential" and "important" entities, whereas CERT-In's directions are broadly applicable to "body corporate" and service providers. The SEC rules are narrower still, focusing exclusively on publicly traded companies. India's framework, particularly when combining CERT-In's broad reach with DPDPA's data fiduciary obligations, often covers a wider array of entities and incident types.

## Practical Takeaway

Indian businesses, General Counsels, and Data Protection Officers must adopt a holistic and proactive approach to incident response. Given the extremely tight reporting timelines under CERT-In and RBI, coupled with the DPDPA's obligations for personal data breaches, swift detection, assessment, and communication protocols are paramount. Organizations should develop integrated incident response plans that cater to the unique requirements of each Indian regulator, while also considering how these align with or diverge from international obligations like NIS2 or SEC rules if they operate globally. This includes clear internal procedures for determining incident type, assessing impact, identifying relevant reporting authorities, and preparing multi-faceted notifications, often concurrently, to avoid non-compliance in any jurisdiction.
