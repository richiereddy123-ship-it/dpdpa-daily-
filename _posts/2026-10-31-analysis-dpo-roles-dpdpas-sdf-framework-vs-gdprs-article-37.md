As India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) solidifies its operational framework, businesses are keenly evaluating their compliance obligations. A critical area of focus is the role of the Data Protection Officer (DPO), particularly for entities designated as 'Significant Data Fiduciaries' (SDFs). This framework presents both parallels and distinct differences when compared to the well-established DPO requirements under the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), particularly Article 37. For an India-first audience navigating a complex global privacy landscape, understanding these nuances is paramount.

## Triggers for Mandatory DPO Appointment

Both the DPDPA and GDPR mandate the appointment of a DPO under specific circumstances, albeit with differing criteria. Under the DPDPA, Section 10(1) empowers the Central Government to notify any Data Fiduciary or class of Data Fiduciaries as an SDF, considering factors such as the volume and sensitivity of personal data processed, and the risk of harm to Data Principals. By October 2026, the specific rules outlining these thresholds are largely in effect, guiding entities on their SDF status. Once designated, an SDF is obligated to appoint a DPO.

In contrast, GDPR Article 37(1) lays out three explicit triggers for mandatory DPO appointment: (a) where processing is carried out by a public authority or body (except for courts acting in their judicial capacity); (b) where the core activities of the controller or processor consist of processing operations which, by virtue of their nature, scope, and/or purposes, require regular and systematic monitoring of data subjects on a large scale; or (c) where the core activities of the controller or processor consist of large-scale processing of special categories of data (Article 9) or data relating to criminal convictions and offences (Article 10).

The DPDPA's approach, anchored in the SDF concept, allows for a more flexible, risk-based determination by the government, potentially adapting to evolving data processing landscapes. GDPR's criteria are more prescriptive, offering clearer, though sometimes narrowly defined, triggers for appointment. The DPDPA's "risk of harm" criterion for SDF designation is broader, potentially encompassing a wider range of activities than GDPR's explicit "large scale" or "special categories" definitions.

## Qualifications, Location, and Independence

A key divergence lies in the DPO's employment status and location. DPDPA Section 10(2) explicitly states that the Data Protection Officer appointed by an SDF "shall be an employee of the Significant Data Fiduciary and be based in India." This is a significant stipulation, ensuring direct accountability and physical presence within the Indian jurisdiction.

The GDPR, while requiring the DPO to have expert knowledge of data protection law and practices (Article 37(5)), does not mandate an employment relationship. Organizations can appoint an external DPO, provided they meet the qualification criteria. Furthermore, GDPR Article 38(3) strongly emphasizes the DPO's independence, requiring them to report directly to the highest management level, not be instructed regarding the exercise of their tasks, and not be dismissed or penalised for performing their duties. While DPDPA Section 10(3) outlines the DPO's responsibilities, it is currently less explicit on specific protections for independence against dismissal or penalization, though the spirit of accountability implies a degree of operational autonomy. The DPDPA's requirement for the DPO to be "based in India" is also stricter than GDPR, which allows DPOs to be located anywhere within the EU.

## Functions and Responsibilities

Despite the differences in appointment criteria and employment status, the core functions of a DPO under both regimes share common ground. DPDPA Section 10(3) broadly outlines the DPO's role as "responsible for ensuring compliance with the provisions of this Act, and act as a point of contact for the Data Principals for the redressal of their grievances under this Act."

GDPR Article 39 provides a more detailed list of tasks, including informing and advising the controller or processor and their employees, monitoring compliance, advising on data protection impact assessments (DPIAs), cooperating with the supervisory authority, and acting as a contact point for the supervisory authority and data subjects. While DPDPA's wording is more concise, the responsibility for "ensuring compliance" implicitly covers many of the advisory and monitoring functions detailed in GDPR. The DPDPA's explicit mention of being a point of contact for Data Principals for grievances underscores a direct channel for data subject rights, similar to GDPR's provision for data subjects to contact the DPO on all issues related to processing of their personal data.

## Interplay with Other Indian Regulations

For Indian businesses, the DPDPA's DPO framework does not operate in a vacuum. Existing sector-specific regulations, such as those from the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) for financial institutions, often mandate compliance officers with overlapping responsibilities. Similarly, the IT (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, already require 'Significant Social Media Intermediaries' to appoint a Grievance Officer, Nodal Contact Person, and Chief Compliance Officer.

While GDPR provides a largely unified data protection framework across the EU, Indian entities must navigate this layered regulatory environment. A DPDPA-mandated DPO will need to coordinate closely with these other compliance roles, ensuring a holistic approach to data governance that satisfies multiple regulatory demands. This creates a unique challenge and opportunity for integrated compliance strategies within Indian organizations.

## Practical Takeaway

Indian businesses, particularly those anticipating SDF designation, must proactively prepare for the DPDPA's DPO requirements. This involves not only assessing potential SDF status based on the notified rules but also planning for the appointment of an India-based employee DPO. Organizations should ensure this individual possesses the requisite legal and practical expertise in data protection. Furthermore, given India's multi-layered regulatory landscape, the DPDPA DPO's role must be integrated with existing compliance functions (e.g., those mandated by RBI or IT Rules) to avoid silos and ensure comprehensive data governance across the enterprise. Establishing clear reporting lines and internal support for the DPO will be crucial for effective compliance and accountability under the DPDPA.
