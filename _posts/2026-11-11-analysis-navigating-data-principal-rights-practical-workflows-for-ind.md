The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA), now fully operational with its accompanying Rules, marks a pivotal shift in India's data governance landscape. For Indian businesses, the focus has moved from understanding the law to implementing robust, practical workflows that honour the rights of data principals. Sections 11 through 14 of the DPDPA lay down these fundamental rights, demanding a proactive and structured approach from every Data Fiduciary.

## Understanding the Core Rights and Obligations

At the heart of the DPDPA are the rights empowering individuals over their personal data. Section 11 grants data principals the **Right to Information** about their personal data, including a summary of the personal data being processed, the processing activities, and the identities of all Data Fiduciaries and Processors with whom the data has been shared. This necessitates clear, accessible mechanisms for individuals to request and receive this information.

Section 12 outlines the **Right to Correction and Erasure** of personal data. Data principals can request the correction of inaccurate or incomplete data, or the erasure of data that is no longer necessary for the purpose for which it was collected, or if consent has been withdrawn. However, this right is not absolute; it is subject to legal requirements for data retention, a crucial point for regulated sectors.

Further, Section 13 establishes the **Right to Grievance Redressal**, mandating that Data Fiduciaries put in place an accessible grievance redressal mechanism. This is the primary channel for data principals to raise concerns or exercise their rights. Finally, Section 14 introduces the **Right to Nominate**, allowing data principals to appoint a nominee who can exercise their rights in the event of their death or incapacity.

## Building Robust Workflows for Data Principal Requests

Implementing these rights requires more than just policy updates; it demands integrated operational workflows. First, companies must establish clear and user-friendly channels for data principals to submit requests—be it through a dedicated web portal, email, or a physical address. Upon receiving a request, the immediate priority is **identity verification**. Given the sensitive nature of personal data, robust procedures are essential to ensure the requester is indeed the data principal, or their authorised nominee, preventing fraudulent access or manipulation. The DPDP Rules are expected to provide specific guidance on acceptable verification methods.

For correction requests under Section 12(1)(a), the workflow should involve internal data teams to verify the accuracy claim, make necessary amendments, and then update all relevant systems. For erasure requests under Section 12(1)(b), a critical step is to assess if the data is still necessary for the original purpose or if any other law mandates its retention. For instance, financial institutions governed by RBI norms, or market intermediaries under SEBI regulations, often have statutory obligations to retain transaction data for several years, which would override an erasure request. Similarly, insurance companies regulated by IRDAI must adhere to specific data retention policies. This intersection of DPDPA with sectoral regulations is a key compliance challenge in India.

## Grievance Redressal and Nominee Management

The DPDPA places significant emphasis on effective grievance redressal. Companies must designate a Data Protection Officer (DPO) or a suitable point of contact, as prescribed by Section 13(1), whose details are readily available. The workflow for grievance handling should include clear acknowledgement, investigation, resolution within prescribed timelines (as per DPDP Rules), and communication of the outcome to the data principal. This internal mechanism is the first line of defence before a data principal escalates to the Data Protection Board of India.

Managing nominee information under Section 14 also requires a structured approach. Companies need a secure system to record nominee details, ensure their validity, and establish protocols for verifying the nominee's identity and the data principal's death or incapacity when a request is made on their behalf. This involves careful documentation and secure storage of nomination forms.

## Interplay with Other Laws and Global Standards

While the DPDPA is the primary framework, its implementation must consider other Indian laws. The IT Rules, 2021, particularly those related to intermediaries, continue to influence how online platforms handle user data and grievances, though the DPDPA now takes precedence for personal data. The DPDPA's approach to data principal rights, especially the right to erasure, is often compared to GDPR's "right to be forgotten." However, the DPDPA's Section 12(1)(b) explicitly provides for exceptions where data retention is necessary for the original purpose or legal compliance, making it less absolute than some interpretations of GDPR. This nuance is vital for Indian businesses, particularly those operating across borders. Furthermore, companies must also consider how these rights apply to personal data collected prior to the DPDPA's full enforcement, ensuring legacy data is brought into compliance.

## Practical Takeaway

Indian businesses, General Counsels, and Data Protection Officers must move beyond theoretical understanding to practical implementation. This involves: (1) mapping all data principal touchpoints; (2) designing and testing clear, secure workflows for identity verification, request processing, and grievance redressal; (3) training staff on these new procedures; (4) integrating DPDPA compliance with existing sectoral regulatory obligations (RBI, SEBI, IRDAI); and (5) investing in technology solutions that facilitate data access, correction, and erasure, while maintaining robust audit trails. Proactive preparation and continuous refinement of these workflows are crucial for fostering trust and ensuring compliance in India's evolving data protection regime.
