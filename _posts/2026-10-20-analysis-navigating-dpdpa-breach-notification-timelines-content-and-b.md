The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA), now fully operational, has fundamentally reshaped India's data protection landscape. Among its most critical provisions are those governing personal data breach notifications, a cornerstone of accountability and transparency. As businesses across sectors grapple with implementation, understanding the nuances of DPDPA's breach notification framework, particularly in conjunction with existing sectoral regulations, is paramount.

## The Obligation to Notify: Who, What, When?

At its core, DPDPA mandates a clear obligation for Data Fiduciaries to report personal data breaches. Section 22(5) of the DPDPA stipulates that in the event of a "personal data breach," the Data Fiduciary must notify "the Board and each affected Data Principal in such form and manner as may be prescribed." This seemingly straightforward requirement opens several practical considerations.

Firstly, the definition of "personal data breach" under Section 2(x) is broad, encompassing any unauthorised processing, accidental disclosure, acquisition, sharing, or alteration of personal data, or accidental loss of access to personal data, that compromises its confidentiality, integrity, or availability. This wide scope means organisations must be vigilant in identifying and assessing incidents.

Secondly, the "when" of notification is crucial. While Section 22(5) defers to the yet-to-be-notified DPDP Rules for specific timelines, global best practices, such as GDPR's 72-hour window for supervisory authorities, suggest that promptness will be a key expectation. For Data Principals, the notification must also be "without undue delay," ensuring they can take protective measures. The DPDP Rules are expected to clarify whether different thresholds or timelines apply to the Board versus individual notifications, possibly differentiating based on the "significant personal data breach" concept (Section 2(x)) that triggers enhanced Board scrutiny.

## Content of the Notification: What Information is Required?

Beyond the timeline, the content of a breach notification is vital for its effectiveness. While the DPDPA itself, in Section 22(5), leaves the specifics to the DPDP Rules, we can anticipate requirements similar to global standards, tailored to the Indian context. A notification to the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) and affected Data Principals will likely need to include:

*   The nature of the personal data breach, including, where possible, the categories and approximate number of Data Principals concerned and the categories and approximate number of personal data records concerned.
*   The likely consequences of the personal data breach.
*   The measures taken or proposed to be taken by the Data Fiduciary to address the personal data breach, including, where appropriate, measures to mitigate its possible adverse effects.
*   The name and contact details of the Data Protection Officer (DPO), if appointed, or another contact point where more information can be obtained.

The DPDP Rules will likely provide templates or guidelines to ensure consistency and clarity. This structured approach will enable the DPBI to effectively assess the incident and provide guidance, while empowering Data Principals to understand the risks and take necessary action. Unlike GDPR Article 34, which allows for notification to Data Principals to be waived under certain conditions (e.g., data encryption), the DPDPA's current wording suggests a more direct obligation, though the Rules may introduce nuances.

## Sectoral Overlays and the DPBI's Role

A significant complexity for Indian businesses lies in reconciling DPDPA's breach notification requirements with existing sectoral regulations. India's regulatory landscape features several well-established frameworks for incident reporting:

*   **RBI**: The Reserve Bank of India's Master Direction on Cyber Security Framework in Banks and IT Framework for NBFCs mandate strict reporting timelines for cyber incidents, often within 6 hours of detection for critical events.
*   **SEBI**: The Securities and Exchange Board of India's Cyber Security and Cyber Resilience frameworks for market intermediaries and infrastructure institutions also prescribe specific incident reporting to SEBI and CERT-In.
*   **IRDAI**: The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India's Guidelines on Information and Cyber Security for Insurers similarly require reporting of security incidents.
*   **IT Rules, 2021**: Rule 3(1)(j) for intermediaries mandates due diligence regarding security incidents, often linking to CERT-In directions.

The DPDPA, under Section 42(1), has an overriding effect on other laws inconsistent with its provisions. However, Section 42(2) clarifies that it does not derogate from any other law that provides a higher degree of protection for Data Principals. This means that where sectoral regulations impose stricter or more immediate reporting obligations (e.g., to sectoral regulators or CERT-In), those will likely continue to apply alongside DPDPA requirements. Data Fiduciaries will thus face a multi-layered reporting obligation, necessitating a coordinated incident response strategy that satisfies all relevant authorities, with the DPBI acting as the central privacy regulator.

## Practicalities of Board Reporting and Beyond

The operationalisation of the DPBI is central to the breach notification process. It is expected that the Board will establish an online portal or a standardised submission mechanism for breach notifications. This portal will likely require structured information, potentially with fields mirroring the anticipated content requirements. The concept of a "significant personal data breach" (Section 2(x)) will be critical here; the DPDP Rules or subsequent DPBI guidelines will define the criteria for significance, which could include the volume of data, sensitivity of the data, potential for harm, and impact on critical infrastructure. Not all personal data breaches will require direct Board notification, but all will require internal documentation and assessment.

Organisations must develop robust internal incident response plans that clearly delineate roles, responsibilities, and communication protocols. This includes a clear escalation path to legal counsel, DPO (if applicable), and senior management. Effective record-keeping of all personal data breaches, even those not deemed "significant" enough for Board notification, is essential for demonstrating accountability and compliance. Furthermore, the DPDPA's emphasis on transparency means that Data Fiduciaries must be prepared for potential public scrutiny and reputational damage following a breach.

**Practical takeaway:** Indian businesses, GCs, and DPOs must proactively establish a comprehensive incident response framework. This framework should integrate DPDPA's notification mandates (to the DPBI and Data Principals) with existing sectoral reporting obligations (to RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, CERT-In, etc.). Develop clear internal protocols for identifying, assessing, containing, and reporting breaches, ensuring compliance with anticipated DPDP Rules timelines and content requirements. Invest in training for staff, robust data mapping, and regular security audits to minimise breach risks and streamline response efforts.
