The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA), now fully operational as of November 2026, has ushered in a new era for data governance in India. For the burgeoning fintech sector, this legislation, coupled with the Reserve Bank of India's (RBI) stringent regulatory framework, presents a complex yet critical compliance landscape. Fintech entities, by their very nature, are data-intensive, handling sensitive personal data ranging from financial transactions to biometric information. Understanding the DPDPA's implications, particularly where it intersects with existing RBI norms, is paramount for sustainable growth and maintaining customer trust.

## DPDPA's Core Mandates for Data Fiduciaries

At its heart, the DPDPA establishes robust obligations for Data Fiduciaries – entities determining the purpose and means of processing personal data, which squarely includes most fintech companies. A cornerstone of the Act is the requirement for explicit, informed, and unambiguous consent from Data Principals (individuals) for processing their personal data (S.6). This moves beyond implied consent, demanding granular control from users, a significant shift for fintechs accustomed to broad terms and conditions. While the DPDPA does outline "legitimate uses" where consent isn't strictly necessary (S.7), these are narrowly defined and primarily cover situations like regulatory compliance, legal obligations, or public interest, not general business operations.

Fintechs must also adhere to principles of purpose limitation and data minimisation, ensuring data is collected only for specified, lawful purposes and is not excessive for that purpose (S.6(1)). Data Fiduciaries are obligated to maintain the accuracy and completeness of personal data (S.8(3)) and implement reasonable security safeguards to prevent breaches (S.8(5)). These duties extend to Data Processors – third-party vendors handling data on behalf of the fintech – for whom the Data Fiduciary remains accountable (S.8(9)). This means fintechs must conduct thorough due diligence and establish robust contractual agreements with their service providers, mirroring existing RBI outsourcing guidelines but with an enhanced privacy lens.

## Harmonising DPDPA with RBI's Sectoral Directives

The interplay between the DPDPA and RBI's extensive directives creates a dual compliance challenge. One key area is data localisation. While RBI has historically mandated strict localisation for payment system data, requiring storage only within India, the DPDPA takes a more nuanced approach to cross-border data transfers (S.16). It permits transfers to specified countries or territories unless restricted by the Central Government. This DPDPA provision, while seemingly more liberal than RBI's stance, does not override existing sectoral mandates. Fintechs must therefore continue to adhere to RBI's localisation requirements for specific data categories, while applying DPDPA's framework for other personal data, ensuring transfers only occur to jurisdictions deemed safe or as permitted by the government.

Another critical intersection is customer protection and grievance redressal. RBI has long emphasised robust customer service and dispute resolution mechanisms. The DPDPA reinforces this by granting Data Principals significant rights, including the right to access information about their data (S.12(1)), the right to correction and erasure (S.12(2)), and the right to grievance redressal (S.13). Fintechs must integrate these DPDPA-mandated rights into their existing customer service frameworks, ensuring timely and effective responses to data access requests or complaints about data processing.

## Emerging Compliance Hotspots for Fintechs

The DPDPA introduces several new compliance obligations that will significantly impact fintech operations. Data breach notification is a prime example. The Act mandates that Data Fiduciaries notify the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) and affected Data Principals in the event of a personal data breach (S.17). This complements RBI's existing cybersecurity incident reporting guidelines, requiring fintechs to streamline their incident response plans to address both regulatory reporting obligations efficiently.

Furthermore, large or high-risk fintech entities may find themselves designated as "Significant Data Fiduciaries" (SDFs) by the Central Government (S.10). This designation brings additional onerous obligations, including the appointment of a Data Protection Officer (DPO) based in India, conducting Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs), and undergoing periodic independent data audits. Given the scale of data processing and the sensitivity of financial data, many prominent fintechs are likely candidates for SDF designation, necessitating proactive planning for these enhanced compliance requirements. The widespread use of artificial intelligence and machine learning for credit scoring, fraud detection, and personalised financial products also demands careful consideration under DPDPA's principles of fairness and transparency, ensuring algorithmic decisions are explainable and do not lead to discriminatory outcomes.

## Practical Takeaway

Indian fintech businesses, General Counsels, and Data Protection Officers must recognise that DPDPA is not merely an IT compliance exercise but a fundamental shift in how personal data is managed. Proactive steps include mapping all personal data flows, implementing robust consent management platforms that capture granular consent, and reviewing existing contracts with Data Processors to ensure DPDPA compliance. Developing integrated policies that harmonise DPDPA's requirements with RBI's existing mandates (e.g., for data localisation, outsourcing, and grievance redressal) is crucial. Investing in employee training, conducting regular data protection impact assessments for new products or services, and preparing for potential SDF obligations are no longer optional but essential for navigating this evolving regulatory landscape and building enduring trust with Indian consumers.
