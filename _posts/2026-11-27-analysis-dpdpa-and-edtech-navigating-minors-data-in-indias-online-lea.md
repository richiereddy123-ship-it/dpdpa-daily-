India's burgeoning edtech sector, a global powerhouse of innovation and learning, now operates under the full ambit of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) and the subsequent DPDP Rules, 2025. While the Act ushers in a new era of data protection for all citizens, its provisions concerning children's data present a particularly stringent and complex compliance challenge for online learning platforms. As of November 2026, edtech companies must demonstrate robust mechanisms to safeguard the privacy of their youngest users, or face significant penalties.

## The DPDPA's Strict Mandate for Children's Data

The DPDPA unequivocally defines a "child" as an individual under the age of eighteen years (Section 2(c)). This universal age threshold, higher than in some other jurisdictions, immediately sets a demanding standard for edtech platforms. The Act places a primary obligation on Data Fiduciaries (DFs) to obtain verifiable consent from a parent or lawful guardian before processing any personal data of a child (Section 10(1)). The DPDP Rules, 2025, are expected to elaborate on the specific methods for such verification, which will be crucial for practical implementation.

Beyond mere consent, the DPDPA imposes explicit prohibitions. Data Fiduciaries are forbidden from undertaking tracking or behavioural monitoring of children, engaging in targeted advertising directed at children, or processing their personal data in any manner that is likely to cause harm to a child's well-being (Section 10(2)). This "harm to well-being" clause is broad and requires careful interpretation by edtech companies, as even seemingly innocuous features like gamification, performance analytics, or personalized content recommendations could, in certain contexts, be construed as detrimental if not designed with utmost care. The Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) is empowered to issue guidelines on these specific aspects (Section 10(3)), which edtech platforms eagerly anticipate.

## Operational Challenges: Verification and Product Design

For edtech companies, the DPDPA's requirements translate into substantial operational and design overhauls. The first hurdle is establishing a robust, verifiable parental consent mechanism. Methods could range from OTP-based verification to a parent's registered mobile number or email, to more advanced digital identity solutions. However, these must be implemented in a user-friendly manner that doesn't create undue friction, yet provides sufficient assurance to the DPBI. Simply relying on self-declaration of age is no longer viable.

The prohibitions on tracking, behavioural monitoring, and targeted advertising necessitate a fundamental rethink of product architecture. Edtech platforms often rely on these very tools to personalize learning experiences, identify areas for improvement, and engage users. Under the DPDPA, any such features for minors must be re-engineered to avoid these prohibited activities. This could mean offering a "child-safe" mode by default, with reduced data collection and no personalization based on behavioural tracking. The "design by default" principle, while not explicitly named in DPDPA, is implicitly demanded for children's data, requiring platforms to prioritize privacy from the outset rather than as an afterthought.

## Intersecting Regulations and Sectoral Nuances

While the DPDPA is the primary legislation, edtech companies must also consider other regulatory frameworks. The Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, for instance, impose due diligence obligations on intermediaries, including measures to prevent the hosting or sharing of child sexual abuse material. While the DPDPA addresses data *processing*, the IT Rules focus on *content*. Together, they create a comprehensive framework for safeguarding children online.

Currently, India lacks a dedicated sectoral regulator for edtech that would issue specific data protection norms, unlike the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) for financial data or IRDAI for insurance. However, the Ministry of Education or other government bodies may issue guidelines or codes of conduct for online learning platforms, which could complement the DPDPA's provisions. Companies should monitor these developments closely. A brief comparison with the GDPR highlights India's stricter approach; while GDPR sets a general age of digital consent at 16 (with member states able to lower to 13), the DPDPA maintains a uniform 18-year threshold across the board, coupled with explicit prohibitions on common data monetization practices.

## Accountability and Compliance Burden

Non-compliance with the DPDPA carries severe financial penalties, with fines for breaches related to children's data potentially reaching up to two hundred crore rupees (Section 33). This underscores the critical need for edtech platforms to establish robust internal governance structures. Many edtech companies will qualify as "Significant Data Fiduciaries" (SDFs) due to the volume and sensitivity of children's data they process, necessitating the appointment of a Data Protection Officer (DPO) (Section 11) and conducting Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) for high-risk processing activities. These measures are not merely compliance checkboxes but fundamental to building trust with parents and ensuring responsible data stewardship.

## Practical Takeaway

For Indian edtech businesses, their General Counsel, and Data Protection Officers, the DPDPA mandates a proactive, privacy-by-design approach to children's data. This involves: (1) **Re-evaluating Consent Mechanisms:** Implement verifiable parental consent processes that are robust yet user-friendly, potentially leveraging government-approved digital identity solutions where feasible. (2) **Redesigning Products for Privacy:** Scrutinize all features related to tracking, behavioural monitoring, and targeted advertising for minors, ensuring they are either eliminated or fundamentally re-engineered to comply with Section 10(2). Prioritize the "child's well-being" in every design decision. (3) **Conducting DPIAs:** Regularly assess the privacy risks associated with processing children's data and implement mitigation strategies. (4) **Training and Awareness:** Ensure all staff, from product developers to customer support, are fully aware of their obligations under the DPDPA regarding children's data. (5) **Engaging with the DPBI:** Stay abreast of any guidelines or clarifications issued by the DPBI, especially concerning age verification and the interpretation of "harm to a child's well-being." The era of unrestricted data collection from minors is over; responsible innovation is now the only path forward.
