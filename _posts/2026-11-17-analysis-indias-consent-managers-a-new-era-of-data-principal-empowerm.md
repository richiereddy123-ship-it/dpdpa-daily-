The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) has ushered in a transformative era for data privacy in India, placing the Data Principal firmly at the centre. A pivotal innovation in this framework is the concept of the Consent Manager, envisioned as a crucial enabler for Data Principals to exercise their rights effectively. As of November 2026, with the DPDPA fully operational and the Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2026 (DPDP Rules) in effect, Consent Managers are no longer a theoretical construct but a burgeoning reality shaping India's digital economy.

## The Business Model and Regulatory Framework for Consent Managers

At its core, a Consent Manager (CM) is an entity registered with the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI), acting as a digital interface for Data Principals to give, manage, review, and withdraw their consent to Data Fiduciaries. This function is explicitly permitted by Section 6(3) of the DPDPA. The DPDP (Consent Managers) Rules, 2026, specifically Rule 4, detail the stringent accreditation and registration process, requiring CMs to meet prescribed technical standards, security protocols, and audit requirements to ensure reliability and independence. These standards are crucial for building trust, especially given the sensitive nature of consent management.

The business model for CMs is primarily service-fee driven, with Data Fiduciaries likely bearing the cost for integrating with and utilising CM platforms. This ensures that CMs remain incentivised to provide robust, neutral services without directly charging Data Principals, thereby promoting accessibility. It's vital that the regulatory framework, particularly Rule 6 of the DPDP (Consent Managers) Rules, 2026, mandates clear separation between the CM's core function and any other commercial interests, safeguarding its independence.

Crucially, while a CM acts as an agent for the Data Principal, it also processes personal data (e.g., identity, consent preferences, transaction logs) to perform its functions. This means a CM itself qualifies as a Data Fiduciary for this operational data. Consequently, CMs are bound by all relevant DPDPA obligations, including adhering to data security standards under Section 9, ensuring data retention limits under Section 10, and establishing a robust grievance redressal mechanism under Section 13.

## Liability and Accountability for Consent Managers

The DPDPA's framework places significant accountability on Consent Managers. As an agent of the Data Principal, a CM’s primary duty is to accurately and promptly reflect the Data Principal's consent decisions. Any failure to do so, such as misrepresenting consent or delaying withdrawal requests, could lead to substantial penalties under Section 33 of the DPDPA. The DPDP (Consent Managers) Rules, 2026, specifically Rule 7, outlines specific liabilities for CMs, including fines for breaches of their fiduciary duty to the Data Principal.

Furthermore, as a Data Fiduciary for the personal data it collects to manage consent, a CM is directly liable for any data breaches or non-compliance with the DPDPA's provisions concerning that data. For instance, a CM that fails to implement reasonable security safeguards as required by Section 9, leading to a compromise of consent records, would face penalties from the DPBI (Section 30). This dual layer of accountability—as an agent of the Data Principal and as a Data Fiduciary for its own operational data—distinguishes India's CM model from mere technical service providers seen in other jurisdictions like under GDPR, where consent management platforms are often processors for data controllers. The Indian model empowers the Data Principal with a dedicated, regulated representative.

## Navigating Interoperability and Sectoral Overlap

A key challenge and opportunity for the CM ecosystem lies in ensuring seamless interoperability. For CMs to truly empower Data Principals, they must be able to communicate effectively with a wide array of Data Fiduciaries across different sectors. Rule 5 of the DPDP (Consent Managers) Rules, 2026, mandates the development of open technical standards and APIs to facilitate this. The DPBI's role in setting and enforcing these standards will be critical to prevent fragmentation and foster a competitive, yet integrated, CM landscape.

Another significant aspect is the integration with existing sectoral consent-driven frameworks. India already has pioneering models like the Account Aggregator (AA) framework regulated by the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), which facilitates consent-based data sharing in the financial sector. The question arises whether existing AA entities will automatically qualify as CMs under DPDPA, or if they will require additional certification and compliance with the DPDP (Consent Managers) Rules, 2026. Similar considerations apply to health data initiatives under the Ayushman Bharat Digital Mission (ABDM) or telecom data sharing norms regulated by TRAI. Harmonising these frameworks, perhaps through mutual recognition or a unified certification process, will be crucial to avoid regulatory friction and provide a consistent experience for Data Principals. Section 17(1)(b) of the DPDPA allows for processing for lawful purposes, but the CM framework is about *managing* consent, which is distinct from the lawful purpose itself.

## Practical Takeaway

For Indian businesses, including Data Fiduciaries, General Counsels, and Data Protection Officers, the rise of Consent Managers signifies a paradigm shift. Data Fiduciaries must proactively engage with accredited CMs, integrate their systems to honour consent signals, and understand their own liabilities when relying on CMs. For entities considering becoming CMs, a thorough understanding of the DPDPA and the DPDP (Consent Managers) Rules, 2026, is paramount, particularly regarding their dual role as a Data Principal's agent and a Data Fiduciary for their operational data. Investing in robust security, transparent practices, and seamless interoperability will be key to success in this evolving regulatory landscape.
