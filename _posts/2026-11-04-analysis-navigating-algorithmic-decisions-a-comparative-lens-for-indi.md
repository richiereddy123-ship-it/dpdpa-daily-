The increasing reliance on algorithmic decision-making (ADM) across sectors, from credit scoring to employment, has brought its regulation to the forefront of global privacy and technology discourse. For Indian businesses operating in this evolving landscape, understanding domestic provisions alongside international benchmarks is crucial. As of November 2026, India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDPA), 2023, provides a foundational framework, but its approach to ADM differs significantly from more prescriptive regimes like the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), the EU AI Act, and the Colorado AI Act.

## DPDPA's Implicit Coverage and Sectoral Specificity

The DPDPA, 2023, does not explicitly define or address "algorithmic decision-making" or "automated individual decision-making" in the manner of its global counterparts. Instead, it relies on overarching principles that implicitly govern the use of algorithms involving personal data. Data Fiduciaries are obligated to process personal data lawfully, fairly, and transparently (Section 4). Consent (Section 7) remains a cornerstone for most processing activities, requiring clear articulation of the purpose for which data is collected. For ADM, this implies that if personal data is used to make automated decisions, the data principal's consent must cover this specific use, or it must fall under one of the "legitimate uses" outlined in Section 8.

Furthermore, the DPDPA mandates Data Fiduciaries to implement reasonable security safeguards to prevent data breaches (Section 10) and holds them accountable for compliance (Section 15). Data principals are also granted rights, including the right to information about personal data processing (Section 11(1)(a)) and the right to correction and erasure (Section 11(1)(b), (c)). While these rights can be leveraged to inquire about or challenge automated decisions, the Act does not provide specific mechanisms for human intervention or contesting such decisions.

Where the DPDPA remains relatively silent, sector-specific regulations in India often fill the void. For instance, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has issued guidelines on digital lending and the use of artificial intelligence/machine learning (AI/ML) in financial services, which impose requirements for transparency, fairness, and consumer protection in automated credit assessments. This highlights India's approach: a broad data protection law complemented by targeted sectoral rules for specific ADM applications. Compared to foreign frameworks, the DPDPA is **looser** and **silent** on explicit ADM rights, placing a greater emphasis on general data protection principles and sectoral regulators.

## GDPR Article 22 and the EU AI Act: Explicit Protections and Comprehensive Governance

In stark contrast, the European Union has adopted a multi-layered approach to ADM. GDPR Article 22 grants data subjects the explicit right not to be subject to a decision based solely on automated processing, including profiling, which produces legal effects concerning them or similarly significantly affects them. This right comes with limited exceptions, such as when necessary for entering into or performance of a contract, authorised by Union or Member State law, or based on explicit consent. Crucially, even in these exceptional cases, suitable safeguards must be in place, including the right to obtain human intervention, to express one's point of view, and to contest the decision. This makes GDPR **stricter** than the DPDPA by providing concrete individual rights against purely automated decisions.

Building upon GDPR, the EU AI Act, which will be largely in force by 2026, introduces a comprehensive, risk-based regulatory framework for AI systems, many of which underpin ADM. The Act categorises AI systems into different risk levels:
*   **Prohibited AI systems**: Those posing an unacceptable risk (e.g., social scoring by public authorities).
*   **High-risk AI systems**: Used in critical areas like employment, credit assessment, law enforcement, and education. These systems face stringent requirements, including conformity assessments, risk management systems, data governance, human oversight, transparency, accuracy, and cybersecurity.
*   **Limited risk AI systems**: Subject to specific transparency obligations (e.g., deepfakes).

For high-risk AI systems driving ADM, the EU AI Act mandates a level of scrutiny and governance far beyond what the DPDPA or even GDPR Article 22 alone requires. It shifts focus from just data protection to the entire lifecycle of AI development and deployment, making it **significantly stricter** and more prescriptive than India's current framework.

## Colorado AI Act: Focusing on Algorithmic Discrimination

Across the Atlantic, the Colorado Artificial Intelligence Act, effective August 2026, presents a significant state-level initiative in the US, focusing specifically on preventing algorithmic discrimination in "high-risk artificial intelligence systems." This Act defines high-risk AI as any AI system that makes or is a substantial factor in making a "consequential decision" about a consumer, which includes decisions related to employment, financial services, housing, insurance, healthcare, and education.

The Colorado AI Act imposes specific duties on both developers and deployers of high-risk AI systems. Developers must exercise reasonable care to ensure their systems do not introduce algorithmic discrimination and provide deployers with necessary documentation. Deployers must implement risk management programs, conduct impact assessments to evaluate and mitigate discriminatory outcomes, and provide clear disclosures to consumers about the use of AI, its purpose, and how to opt out of or appeal a consequential decision made by a high-risk AI system. They also require annual independent audits.

While the DPDPA's general principles might implicitly discourage discrimination, the Colorado AI Act is **stricter** by explicitly targeting algorithmic discrimination and mandating concrete steps like impact assessments, risk management, and consumer rights to opt-out and appeal. It shares the risk-based approach of the EU AI Act but is particularly honed in on fairness and non-discrimination in ADM.

## Practical takeaway

For Indian businesses, the DPDPA's relative silence on explicit ADM rights should not be interpreted as a carte blanche. The Act's foundational principles of consent (Section 7), purpose limitation, accountability (Section 15), and data principal rights (Section 11) inherently apply to any algorithmic processing of personal data. Moreover, sector-specific regulations, like those from the RBI, demonstrate a clear intent to regulate ADM where risks are high. Businesses developing or deploying ADM, especially those operating globally, should proactively align with emerging international standards. This means moving beyond mere DPDPA compliance to consider implementing robust AI governance frameworks, conducting algorithmic impact assessments, ensuring transparency, explainability, and human oversight for consequential automated decisions, and establishing clear mechanisms for data principals to understand and contest ADM outcomes. Adopting best practices from GDPR, the EU AI Act, and the Colorado AI Act will not only mitigate future compliance risks but also foster trust and responsible innovation.
