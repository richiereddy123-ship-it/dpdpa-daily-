As India's digital economy continues its rapid ascent, particularly in fintech, understanding the nuanced interplay of its privacy regulations against global benchmarks is crucial. Today, November 14, 2026, with the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) firmly in effect, Indian businesses operate under a robust framework, complemented by the Reserve Bank of India's (RBI) stringent Digital Lending Guidelines (DLG), 2022. This analysis anchors on these Indian provisions, comparing them with the US Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA) and the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) alongside the Payment Services Directive 2 (PSD2).

## Foundational Principles: Consent and Purpose Limitation

At the heart of modern data protection lies consent and the principle of purpose limitation. The DPDPA, in Section 6, mandates clear, affirmative, and unambiguous consent from the Data Principal for the specified purpose of processing their personal data. This aligns closely with the GDPR's Article 4(11) and Article 7 requirements for "freely given, specific, informed and unambiguous" consent, with an easy mechanism for withdrawal (GDPR Article 7(3), DPDPA Section 7). The RBI DLG reinforces this for digital lending, requiring explicit consent for need-based data collection (Para 6.1) and allowing borrowers to revoke consent at any time (Para 6.4).

In contrast, the US GLBA, primarily enacted in 1999, adopts a comparatively looser approach. Its Privacy Rule (15 U.S.C. § 6802(b)) largely operates on an opt-out model for sharing "nonpublic personal information" (NPI) with non-affiliated third parties, rather than demanding affirmative consent upfront. While financial institutions must provide clear privacy notices, the onus is often on the consumer to decline data sharing, making the Indian and EU regimes stricter on the initiation of data processing.

## Data Minimisation and Collection Scrutiny

The principle of data minimisation – collecting only what is necessary for the stated purpose – is a critical differentiator. DPDPA Section 6(1)(a) implicitly requires data to be processed only for the purpose for which consent was given, implying minimisation. The RBI DLG, however, takes an exceptionally granular and stricter stance. Para 6.1 explicitly limits data collection by digital lenders to "need-based" information, strictly prohibiting access to contact lists, call logs, or media galleries (Para 6.1.2). Furthermore, one-time access to mobile camera, microphone, or location is permitted only with explicit consent and for specific, justifiable purposes (Para 6.1.1).

The GDPR (Article 5(1)(c)) also mandates that personal data be "adequate, relevant and limited to what is necessary," a principle strongly echoed by PSD2 for payment service providers (PSPs). PSD2, for instance, allows Account Information Service Providers (AISPs) and Payment Initiation Service Providers (PISPs) to access only data necessary for the service requested by the user, and only with explicit consent (Articles 66(4), 67(2)). The GLBA, while requiring financial institutions to safeguard NPI, is less prescriptive on data minimisation at the point of collection, relying more on internal security programs (Safeguards Rule, 16 CFR Part 314) rather than strict limits on *what* data can be collected in the first place.

## Data Subject Rights and Accountability

Both the DPDPA and GDPR empower individuals with extensive rights over their data. DPDPA Chapter III grants Data Principals rights such as access to information, correction, erasure, and grievance redressal. Similarly, GDPR provides comprehensive rights including access, rectification, erasure (the "right to be forgotten"), data portability, and objection to processing (Articles 15-22). Both frameworks also impose significant accountability on data fiduciaries/controllers, including breach notification requirements (DPDPA Section 8(6), GDPR Articles 33-34) and, for significant entities, the appointment of a Data Protection Officer (DPDPA Section 10, GDPR Article 37). The RBI DLG further mandates a dedicated grievance redressal officer for digital lenders (Para 6.5).

The GLBA offers more limited individual rights. While consumers have the right to receive privacy notices, they generally lack statutory rights to access, correct, or erase their NPI held by financial institutions. Accountability under GLBA focuses more on establishing and maintaining reasonable security safeguards (Safeguards Rule) rather than the prescriptive DPO roles or impact assessments seen in India and the EU. This makes the Indian and EU frameworks significantly more robust in empowering individuals and holding organisations accountable.

## Cross-Border Data Flows and Localisation

Cross-border data transfer rules present another area of divergence. DPDPA Section 16 permits the transfer of personal data outside India to such countries or territories as may be notified by the Central Government, offering a framework similar to GDPR's adequacy decisions (GDPR Article 45). However, the RBI DLG introduces a stricter, sector-specific localisation requirement, mandating that all data collected by Regulated Entities (REs) and their Lending Service Providers (LSPs) must be stored in India (Para 6.2).

The GDPR (Chapter V) has stringent rules for transferring personal data outside the EU, requiring mechanisms like adequacy decisions, Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs), or Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs). PSD2, being an EU directive, operates within the GDPR's cross-border transfer framework. The GLBA, on the other hand, is largely silent on data localisation or specific cross-border transfer mechanisms, leaving it to broader contractual agreements and general US legal principles, making it considerably looser in this regard compared to the explicit requirements in India and the EU.

## Practical Takeaway

For Indian businesses, particularly those in fintech, navigating this landscape requires a multi-faceted compliance strategy. The DPDPA sets the baseline for personal data protection, but the RBI DLG imposes significantly stricter, sector-specific obligations, especially regarding data collection, storage, and consent granularity. When engaging with global partners or expanding internationally, understanding the opt-out nature of GLBA versus the explicit consent models of GDPR/PSD2 is critical. While Indian law is stricter on data minimisation and localisation for digital lending, its DPDPA framework for data principal rights and accountability aligns closely with global best practices like GDPR. Businesses must ensure their data governance models are adaptable, capable of meeting the stringent consent and minimisation demands of India and the EU, while also addressing the specific security and notice requirements of the US. A 'one-size-fits-all' approach to privacy will inevitably fall short.
