The global landscape of data privacy enforcement is increasingly complex, with jurisdictions worldwide establishing robust frameworks to protect personal data. For Indian businesses, understanding the powers and penalty structures of the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) under the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDP Act), alongside other relevant Indian regulations like those from the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) and the Information Technology (IT) Rules, is paramount. This analysis anchors on the Indian regime, comparing its enforcement mechanisms against those of leading international authorities: France's CNIL (Commission Nationale de l'Informatique et des Libertés) under the GDPR, the UK's ICO (Information Commissioner's Office) under the UK GDPR, and the US Federal Trade Commission (FTC).

## Monetary Penalties: Structure and Scale

India's DPDP Act outlines a clear structure for monetary penalties, with significant fines for non-compliance. Under Section 33(2) of the DPDP Act, the maximum penalty for failing to adopt reasonable security safeguards to prevent a personal data breach is up to INR 250 crore. Other violations, such as failure to notify the DPBI and affected Data Principals of a personal data breach, can attract penalties up to INR 200 crore (Section 33(2)(c)). The DPBI determines the penalty amount based on factors enumerated in Section 34, including the nature and severity of the breach, the number of Data Principals affected, and whether remedial action was taken. This fixed monetary ceiling provides a degree of predictability but contrasts sharply with the percentage-based turnover fines seen elsewhere.

In the European Union, the GDPR, enforced by bodies like the CNIL, employs a two-tiered penalty system under Article 83. Less severe infringements can result in fines up to €10 million or 2% of the company's total worldwide annual turnover from the preceding financial year, whichever is higher. More serious violations, such as breaches of core data processing principles or Data Principal rights, can incur penalties up to €20 million or 4% of global annual turnover. The UK GDPR, enforced by the ICO, mirrors this structure (Article 83 UK GDPR), with maximum fines of £8.7 million/2% and £17.5 million/4% respectively. These percentage-based fines often lead to significantly higher penalties for large multinational corporations compared to India's fixed ceiling.

The US FTC's approach to penalties is generally more varied and often tied to specific statutes. For instance, violations of the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA) can lead to civil penalties of up to tens of thousands of dollars per violation. Under Section 5 of the FTC Act, which prohibits unfair or deceptive acts or practices, the FTC can seek monetary relief, including consumer redress or disgorgement of ill-gotten gains, often through consent decrees or court orders. Unlike the GDPR or DPDP Act, there isn't a single overarching data protection law with a uniform, fixed or percentage-based penalty structure for general data privacy violations. This makes the FTC's enforcement less predictable in terms of maximum potential fines for a broad range of privacy infringements.

## Enforcement Powers and Remedial Actions

Beyond monetary penalties, enforcement bodies wield diverse powers to ensure compliance. The DPBI, under Section 28 of the DPDP Act, can conduct inquiries and issue directions to Data Fiduciaries and Data Processors. Section 31 grants the DPBI powers to direct remedial actions, including measures to mitigate harm, cease processing, or conduct data audits. While not explicitly mirroring the GDPR's power to impose a temporary or permanent ban on processing, the DPBI's ability to issue "directions necessary to ensure compliance" offers a broad scope for intervention.

The CNIL and ICO possess extensive enforcement powers under Article 58 of the GDPR/UK GDPR. These include issuing warnings and reprimands, ordering controllers to comply with data subject requests, imposing temporary or permanent bans on processing, ordering rectification or erasure of data, and requiring notification of data breaches to Data Principals. They can also conduct comprehensive audits and access all personal data and information necessary for their investigations. These powers are generally more explicitly defined and broader in scope regarding proactive intervention in processing activities compared to the DPDP Act.

The FTC's enforcement powers primarily revolve around bringing actions in federal court to stop unlawful practices, obtain consumer redress, and impose civil penalties. Its focus is often on injunctive relief and consent decrees, which are legally binding agreements outlining future compliance measures. While effective in stopping harmful practices and compensating consumers, the FTC generally lacks the explicit, broad audit and proactive directive powers over data processing operations that are characteristic of European data protection authorities.

## Scope of Enforcement and Sectoral Specificity

India's enforcement landscape is multi-layered. The DPDP Act provides a general framework for personal data protection, applicable to all Data Fiduciaries and Data Processors. However, sector-specific regulations, such as those issued by the RBI, introduce additional layers of enforcement. For example, RBI directives on data localisation for payment system operators carry their own penalty structures and can lead to operational restrictions for non-compliant entities. Furthermore, the Information Technology (Intermediary Guidelines and Digital Media Ethics Code) Rules, 2021, and subsequent amendments, confer powers upon the Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) to issue directions to intermediaries, including blocking content or demanding information, with non-compliance potentially leading to penalties under the broader IT Act, 2000. This layered approach means Indian businesses must navigate multiple regulatory bodies and their distinct enforcement mechanisms.

CNIL and ICO enforcement primarily operates under the unified GDPR/UK GDPR framework, applying broadly to all controllers and processors across sectors, with specific provisions for certain sensitive data categories. Accountability (Article 5(2) GDPR) is a central principle, placing the onus on organisations to demonstrate compliance.

The FTC's enforcement, while broad under Section 5, is also supplemented by specific laws like COPPA for children's data or the Gramm-Leach-Bliley Act (GLBA) for financial institutions. This results in a somewhat fragmented approach, where specific privacy issues are addressed through targeted legislation or broad consumer protection mandates, rather than a single, comprehensive data protection authority.

## Practical Takeaway

For Indian businesses, general counsels, and Data Protection Officers, the current regulatory environment demands a sophisticated and multi-faceted compliance strategy. The DPDP Act, with its fixed but substantial penalties and the DPBI's broad investigative and directive powers, forms the bedrock. However, it is crucial to overlay this with an understanding of sector-specific regulations, particularly those from the RBI for financial entities, and the IT Rules for intermediaries. Businesses operating globally must also be acutely aware of the higher percentage-based penalties and explicit proactive enforcement powers of authorities like the CNIL and ICO under GDPR. Developing robust internal governance frameworks, conducting regular data audits, and ensuring prompt breach notification capabilities are not merely best practices but essential safeguards against a complex and increasingly stringent global enforcement landscape.
