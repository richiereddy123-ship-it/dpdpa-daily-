The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA), along with the recently notified DPDP Rules, 2025, marks a transformative era for data privacy in India. As Indian businesses navigate this new regulatory landscape, it’s prudent to look at global precedents, particularly the enforcement trends under the European Union’s General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). While DPDPA has its unique Indian flavour and context, the fundamental principles of data protection and the challenges of compliance are strikingly similar. Learning from GDPR enforcement can help Indian companies pre-empt common pitfalls and build robust privacy frameworks under the DPDPA.

## The Imperative of Granular Consent and Notice

One of the most significant areas of GDPR enforcement has been consent. European regulators have consistently penalised companies for vague, bundled, or non-specific consent mechanisms. This lesson is directly applicable to the DPDPA. Section 6 of the DPDPA mandates that personal data can only be processed for a lawful purpose for which the Data Principal has given consent. Furthermore, Section 5 requires a clear and itemised notice describing the personal data to be collected, the purpose of processing, and how Data Principals can exercise their rights.

Indian companies must move beyond generic "I agree to all terms" checkboxes. Consent under DPDPA needs to be specific, informed, and unambiguous. This means clearly delineating different processing purposes (e.g., marketing, service improvement, analytics) and allowing Data Principals to consent to each purpose separately. Failure to provide a clear notice or obtain granular consent could lead to significant penalties from the Data Protection Board of India.

## Data Minimisation and Purpose Limitation: Beyond Collection

GDPR enforcement has highlighted that collecting excessive data or using collected data for purposes beyond the initial disclosure is a major compliance risk. The DPDPA, while not explicitly using the term "data minimisation," inherently promotes this through its emphasis on processing for a "specified, clear and lawful purpose" (Section 6(1)(a)) and the requirement that data collection be "only to the extent necessary for such purpose" (Section 6(1)(b)).

Indian businesses, across sectors from finance (governed by RBI norms) to healthcare, must meticulously audit their data collection practices. Is every piece of data collected truly necessary for the stated purpose? Data retention policies also fall under this umbrella; data should not be kept longer than necessary, aligning with the "purpose served" principle. Companies must establish clear data retention schedules, which may also need to align with sectoral regulations (e.g., RBI guidelines on data storage for financial transactions). This proactive approach will prevent the accumulation of unnecessary data, reducing both compliance risk and potential breach impact.

## Accountability and Proactive Risk Management

The DPDPA places a strong emphasis on the accountability of Data Fiduciaries (Section 8). While the Act doesn't explicitly mandate Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs) in the same prescriptive manner as GDPR, the spirit of accountability and the requirement to implement "reasonable security safeguards" (Section 9) necessitates a proactive risk assessment approach. GDPR enforcement has shown that companies failing to assess and mitigate privacy risks before embarking on new data processing activities are vulnerable to fines.

Indian companies, particularly Significant Data Fiduciaries as defined under DPDPA, should adopt a robust framework for identifying and mitigating privacy risks. This involves conducting assessments similar to DPIAs for high-risk processing activities, new product launches, or significant changes to data handling practices. Such assessments demonstrate due diligence and a commitment to protecting Data Principal rights, which will be crucial for proving accountability to the Data Protection Board. Sectoral regulators like SEBI or IRDAI may also issue specific guidelines for risk management concerning investor or policyholder data.

## Data Breach Preparedness and Prompt Notification

GDPR enforcement has seen numerous penalties levied for delayed or inadequate data breach notifications. The DPDPA, under Section 10, mandates that Data Fiduciaries notify the Data Protection Board and affected Data Principals in the event of a personal data breach, in a "form and manner as may be prescribed" by the DPDP Rules, 2025. This requirement complements existing breach reporting obligations under the Information Technology (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules, 2011, and CERT-In directives.

Indian companies must develop comprehensive data breach response plans that integrate DPDPA requirements with existing IT Rules. This includes establishing clear protocols for breach detection, assessment, containment, and notification. The focus should be on promptness and providing clear, actionable information to affected Data Principals. A well-rehearsed incident response plan is not just about compliance; it's about maintaining trust and mitigating potential harm.

### Practical takeaway

For Indian businesses, General Counsels, and Data Protection Officers, the time for proactive measures is now. Conduct thorough data mapping exercises to understand where personal data resides and how it’s processed. Review and revamp consent mechanisms to ensure granularity and clarity. Implement robust data minimisation and retention policies. Develop a framework for privacy risk assessments, akin to DPIAs, for new projects and high-risk processing. Finally, bolster your data breach response plan, ensuring it aligns with DPDPA notification requirements and existing CERT-In guidelines. Learning from GDPR's enforcement journey can save Indian companies significant compliance headaches and penalties down the line.
