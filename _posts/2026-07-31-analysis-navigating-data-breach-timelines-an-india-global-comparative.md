As of July 31, 2026, India’s Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDPA), 2023, is fully operational, with its accompanying rules finalized and in effect. This marks a pivotal moment for data protection in India, bringing a structured approach to breach notification that warrants a close comparative look against established global frameworks like the EU’s GDPR, Singapore’s PDPA, and Australia’s Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme. Understanding these varied timelines and triggers is crucial for any entity handling personal data, especially those operating across borders.

## India's Multi-Layered Breach Notification Framework

India's approach to data breach notification is characterized by a dual obligation under the DPDPA, supplemented by stringent sectoral mandates. Under DPDPA Section 17(1), a Data Fiduciary is obligated to notify the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) and affected Data Principals of a personal data breach "in such form and manner as may be prescribed." The DPDP Rules, 2026, now clarify these specifics. They mandate notification to the DPBI "without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours" of becoming aware of a breach. For Data Principals, notification is required "without undue delay" if the breach is likely to result in a significant risk to their rights and freedoms.

Beyond the DPDPA, India has pre-existing, often stricter, reporting requirements. The Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In), under the Information Technology (The Indian Computer Emergency Response Team and Manner of Performing Functions and Duties) Rules, 2021, mandates reporting of cyber incidents, including data breaches, within a mere six hours of noticing or being brought to notice. Similarly, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) directs regulated entities like banks and NBFCs to report cyber incidents to the RBI within six hours of detection. This creates a complex, multi-regulator reporting landscape, often with exceptionally tight deadlines, making India uniquely stringent in certain contexts.

## The EU GDPR: A Global Benchmark for Timeliness

The European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) sets a widely adopted standard for breach notification. Under GDPR Article 33, data controllers must notify the relevant supervisory authority (e.g., a national Data Protection Authority) "without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours" after becoming aware of a personal data breach. This 72-hour clock is a direct inspiration for the DPDPA Rules' timeline for the DPBI.

For affected data subjects, GDPR Article 34 requires notification "without undue delay" if the personal data breach is likely to result in a high risk to their rights and freedoms. This "high risk" threshold means not every breach requires individual notification, a nuance that the DPDPA Rules also incorporate with their "significant risk" trigger for Data Principals. Compared to India, the GDPR's general 72-hour timeline is similar to the DPDPA's requirement for the DPBI, but India's sectoral mandates (CERT-In, RBI) are significantly stricter at six hours for specific incident types and entities.

## Singapore and Australia: Variations in Approach

Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA) offers a slightly different emphasis. Under Section 26C, organizations must notify the Personal Data Protection Commission (PDPC) and affected individuals if a data breach is likely to result in "significant harm" to an individual or is of "significant scale" (e.g., affecting 500 or more individuals). The timeline for notification is "as soon as practicable" and "without undue delay." While the PDPC generally expects notification within three calendar days (72 hours) of completing the breach assessment, the "as soon as practicable" language allows for some flexibility in the initial assessment phase before the clock truly starts ticking on the formal notification.

Australia's Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme, under the Privacy Act 1988 (Section 26WL), requires entities to notify the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) and affected individuals "as soon as practicable" if there are "reasonable grounds to believe" that an "eligible data breach" has occurred. An "eligible data breach" involves unauthorized access or disclosure of personal information that is likely to result in "serious harm" to any individual. Similar to Singapore, the "as soon as practicable" standard provides more leeway than a strict 72-hour deadline from discovery, focusing on the completion of a reasonable assessment. India's DPDPA Rules, with their explicit 72-hour timeline for the DPBI, appear stricter than the general "as soon as practicable" standards in both Singapore and Australia, particularly when considering the 6-hour sectoral requirements.

## Key Differentiators and Indian Context

The Indian regime, particularly with the combined force of the DPDPA Rules and existing sectoral regulations, presents a unique challenge. The 6-hour reporting requirement under CERT-In and RBI for specific cyber incidents is an outlier globally, making India's framework exceptionally stringent for regulated sectors. While the DPDPA's 72-hour timeline for the DPBI aligns with global best practices like GDPR, the concurrent obligation to notify CERT-In within 6 hours for many types of data breaches means that Indian entities, especially those in critical sectors, must operate with unparalleled speed in detection and initial reporting.

Furthermore, the DPDPA Rules' "significant risk" threshold for notifying Data Principals mirrors the "high risk" in GDPR and "serious/significant harm" in Australia/Singapore, indicating a move towards risk-based notification for individuals. However, the multi-regulator reporting burden in India—DPBI, CERT-In, RBI, and potentially others—adds a layer of complexity not typically seen in jurisdictions where a single data protection authority is the primary recipient of breach notifications.

## Practical Takeaway

For Indian businesses, General Counsels, and Data Protection Officers, the operationalization of the DPDPA Rules necessitates a comprehensive overhaul of incident response protocols. It is no longer sufficient to have a generic plan; specific timelines for different regulators must be meticulously integrated. Prioritize the development of robust, multi-faceted incident response plans that account for the 6-hour CERT-In/RBI deadlines, the 72-hour DPBI deadline, and the "without undue delay" requirement for Data Principals in high-risk scenarios. Invest in rapid detection, forensic capabilities, and clear internal escalation paths to ensure timely assessment and reporting. Given the multi-jurisdictional nature of many businesses, harmonizing these strict Indian requirements with global standards is paramount to ensure compliance and maintain trust with Data Principals and regulators alike.
