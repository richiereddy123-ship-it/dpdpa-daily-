As October 26, 2026 dawns, India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) is now firmly entrenched, shaping how Indian businesses handle personal data. While our domestic legal framework, complemented by the recent Digital Personal Data Protection (Removal of Difficulties) Order, 2026, carves out a distinct path, global privacy developments, particularly GDPR enforcement trends, offer invaluable foresight. Indian companies, from startups to conglomerates, must proactively address common pitfalls observed under the GDPR to avoid similar scrutiny from the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI).

## The Imperative of Valid Consent and Lawful Processing

One of the most frequent grounds for GDPR penalties has been a failure to secure valid consent or establish a clear lawful basis for processing. Under the DPDPA, this is equally paramount. Section 6 mandates that personal data can only be processed with the Data Principal's consent, which must be "free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous." The recent "Removal of Difficulties" Order, 2026, has further clarified that consent mechanisms must be granular and easily withdrawable, echoing GDPR's Article 7. Indian businesses must move beyond passive acceptance or pre-ticked boxes. Consent notices need to be presented in clear, plain language, easily accessible, and specify the exact purpose of processing, as required by Section 6(1)(a). Furthermore, companies should rigorously assess if their processing activities fall under one of the "legitimate uses" outlined in Section 7, such as for employment purposes or public interest, rather than defaulting to potentially invalid consent.

## Robust Security Measures and Prompt Breach Notification

GDPR enforcement has repeatedly highlighted inadequate technical and organisational security measures leading to data breaches. The DPDPA places a clear obligation on Data Fiduciaries to implement "reasonable security safeguards" to prevent personal data breaches (Section 9(1)). This isn't merely a theoretical requirement; it demands a proactive approach to cybersecurity, including encryption, access controls, and regular vulnerability assessments. For entities designated as 'Significant Data Fiduciaries' under Section 10(1), these obligations are even more stringent, potentially requiring independent data audits as clarified by the 2026 Order. Crucially, Section 9(4) mandates that in the event of a personal data breach, the Data Fiduciary must notify the DPBI and affected Data Principals "in such form and manner as may be prescribed." Learning from GDPR's strict 72-hour notification window, Indian companies must establish robust internal incident response plans to ensure timely compliance, integrating with existing sectoral norms from regulators like RBI, SEBI, and IRDAI which often prescribe specific cybersecurity standards.

## Operationalising Data Principal Rights

The DPDPA, much like the GDPR, empowers individuals with significant rights over their personal data. GDPR enforcement has shown that failing to honour these rights – such as access, rectification, or erasure – can lead to substantial fines. Indian businesses must be prepared to operationalise the rights of Data Principals enshrined in Chapter III of the DPDPA. This includes the right to obtain information about processing (Section 11), the right to correction and erasure of personal data (Section 12), and the right to grievance redressal (Section 13). Companies need clear, accessible mechanisms for Data Principals to exercise these rights, including designated Grievance Officers. Furthermore, the right to nominate (Section 14) requires companies to have processes for handling such nominations effectively. Simply having a privacy policy is insufficient; the underlying systems and processes must be capable of responding to these requests efficiently and within prescribed timelines.

## Accountability, Data Minimisation, and Purpose Limitation

A recurring theme in GDPR penalties is a lack of demonstrable accountability, including insufficient record-keeping and processing data beyond its stated purpose. The DPDPA implicitly encourages these principles. Section 5 states that personal data can only be processed for a "lawful purpose," and Section 6(1)(a) requires consent for a "specified purpose." This underpins the principles of data minimisation and purpose limitation – collecting only necessary data and using it only for the agreed-upon purpose. Data Fiduciaries, particularly Significant Data Fiduciaries, must maintain comprehensive records of processing activities and demonstrate compliance with the DPDPA, aligning with the audit provisions clarified by the 2026 Order. This proactive stance on accountability and data governance, including adherence to IT Rules, 2021 where applicable, will be critical to avoid regulatory action.

Practical takeaway: Indian businesses, General Counsels, and Data Protection Officers must view the DPDPA not as a mere checklist, but as a fundamental shift in data governance. Proactive investment in clear consent mechanisms, robust cybersecurity infrastructure, efficient data principal rights management systems, and demonstrable accountability through internal audits and record-keeping will be key. Learning from GDPR's enforcement history provides a clear roadmap to navigate the DPDPA landscape successfully and avoid costly penalties.
