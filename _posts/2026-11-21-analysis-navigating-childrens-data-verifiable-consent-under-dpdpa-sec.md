The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA), now fully operational as of November 2026, marks a significant shift in India's data privacy landscape. Among its most impactful provisions is Section 9, dedicated to the protection of children's personal data. This section places a stringent obligation on Data Fiduciaries (DFs) to obtain "verifiable parental consent" before processing any data belonging to individuals under 18 years of age, as defined under Section 2(c) of the Act. While the intent is unequivocally to safeguard minors in the digital realm, the practical implementation of "verifiable" consent presents a complex challenge, particularly within India's diverse socio-economic and technological context.

## The Mandate of DPDPA Section 9

At its core, DPDPA Section 9(1) prohibits Data Fiduciaries from processing the personal data of a child without obtaining the verifiable consent of their parent or lawful guardian. This goes beyond mere age-gating or self-declaration, demanding a concrete mechanism to confirm the identity and authority of the consenting adult. The Act also explicitly prohibits DFs from undertaking specific activities that could be detrimental to children, such as tracking, behavioural monitoring, or targeted advertising (Section 9(2)). Furthermore, DPDPA Section 9(3) outlines limited exceptions where the Central Government may exempt certain classes of DFs from these requirements, primarily for purposes deemed necessary for the safety of children or for specified legitimate purposes, subject to prescribed conditions. This framework establishes one of the most robust protections for children's data globally, arguably even more prescriptive than GDPR's Article 8, which often relies on "reasonable efforts" to verify parental consent.

## The Indian Conundrum: Verifying Parental Consent

The real test of DPDPA Section 9 lies in how Data Fiduciaries will implement "verifiable" consent across India. Given the vast disparities in digital literacy, internet access, and identification documentation, a one-size-fits-all approach is impractical. The DPDP Rules, which are now providing further clarity, are anticipated to specify acceptable methods. Potential mechanisms for verification could include:

*   **Digital Identity Verification**: For digitally savvy parents, integration with existing digital identity frameworks, such as Aadhaar-based OTP verification (albeit with careful consideration of privacy implications and DPDPA's general stance against mandatory Aadhaar linking), or leveraging platforms like DigiLocker for document verification could be explored.
*   **e-KYC Integration**: Entities already bound by RBI or SEBI norms, such as financial institutions or investment platforms, have established e-KYC processes for adults. These could potentially be adapted to verify parental identity for services involving minors, ensuring compliance with both sectoral regulations and DPDPA.
*   **Hybrid Models**: For services requiring lower assurance or catering to less digitally enabled populations, a combination of self-declaration by the parent followed by a secondary verification step (e.g., a small transaction, an email confirmation, or even a brief video call) might be considered, provided the DPDP Rules permit such flexibility.
*   **Consent Managers**: DPDPA Section 6 introduces the concept of Consent Managers, entities registered with the Data Protection Board of India (DPBI) to facilitate consent management. These managers could play a crucial role in streamlining and standardising verifiable parental consent mechanisms, offering a trusted intermediary for both parents and DFs.

The challenge is to devise methods that are secure, scalable, inclusive, and do not create undue friction for legitimate services or exclude segments of the population.

## Sectoral Nuances and Regulatory Overlap

The DPDPA's requirements for children's data do not operate in a vacuum. Various sectoral regulators already have norms concerning minors. For instance:

*   **Financial Services**: RBI guidelines mandate specific procedures for opening bank accounts or making investments for minors, typically requiring guardian consent and KYC. DPDPA Section 9 adds a layer of data protection compliance, ensuring that the personal data collected during these processes is handled with heightened care.
*   **Ed-tech and Online Learning**: India's booming ed-tech sector frequently processes children's data. Beyond the IT Rules, 2021, which address intermediary responsibilities for children's safety, DPDPA Section 9 mandates verifiable parental consent for any data processing, from enrolment details to learning analytics.
*   **Online Gaming and Social Media**: These platforms, often popular with minors, face the most significant challenge. Robust age-gating combined with stringent verifiable consent mechanisms will be critical to prevent violations and ensure children are not exposed to harmful content or targeted advertising.

Data Fiduciaries must meticulously review their existing practices, integrating DPDPA Section 9 requirements with their obligations under sectoral regulations to ensure comprehensive compliance.

## Practical Takeaway

Indian businesses, General Counsels, and Data Protection Officers must urgently operationalise robust mechanisms for verifiable parental consent. This involves more than just updating privacy policies. It requires investing in appropriate age-gating and identity verification technologies, potentially leveraging the emerging ecosystem of Consent Managers under DPDPA Section 6. A thorough audit of all data processing activities involving individuals under 18 years is imperative. Engagement with sectoral regulators for specific guidance and a proactive approach to documenting every step of the consent process will be crucial to mitigate risks and demonstrate compliance with India's pioneering data protection law.
