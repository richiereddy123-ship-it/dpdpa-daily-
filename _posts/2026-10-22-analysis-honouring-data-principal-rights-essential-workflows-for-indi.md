The Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA), now firmly established in India's regulatory landscape as of October 2026, places significant emphasis on the rights of the 'Data Principal'. For Indian businesses, moving beyond theoretical understanding to practical implementation of these rights is paramount. Sections 11 to 14 of the DPDPA outline these fundamental entitlements, and robust, auditable workflows are no longer optional but a legal imperative.

## Understanding the Core Rights and Their Implications

At its heart, the DPDPA empowers individuals with greater control over their digital personal data. Section 11 grants Data Principals the right to access information about their personal data, including a summary of the data being processed, the processing activities, and the identities of all Data Fiduciaries (DFs) with whom their data has been shared. This foundational right necessitates transparent privacy policies and easily accessible channels for DPs to make such inquiries.

Building on this, Section 12 provides Data Principals with the right to correction, completion, updating, and erasure of their personal data. This is a critical provision that demands sophisticated data governance. It’s important to note that this right is not absolute; Section 17(1) clarifies that personal data may continue to be processed if necessary for compliance with any legal obligation or for the exercise or defence of legal claims. This often comes into play when sectoral regulations mandate data retention.

Section 13 establishes the right to grievance redressal, ensuring DPs have a clear path to address any concerns regarding the processing of their data. Finally, Section 14 introduces the unique right to nominate, allowing a Data Principal to designate another individual to exercise their rights in the event of their death or incapacity. This requires DFs to have procedures for verifying such nominations.

## Establishing Robust Workflows for Access and Correction

To honour the rights under Sections 11 and 12, Indian companies must design clear, efficient, and documented workflows. The first step involves creating accessible channels for Data Principals to submit requests – this could be a dedicated web portal, an email address, or a toll-free number. Upon receiving a request for information (Section 11) or correction/update (Section 12), the immediate priority is identity verification. This is crucial to prevent fraudulent requests and protect the Data Principal's privacy. DPDP Rules, likely to be detailed on this by now, will specify acceptable verification methods.

Once verified, the request must be routed internally to the relevant data custodians. For a correction request, this involves locating all instances of the Data Principal's data across various systems (CRM, ERP, marketing databases, etc.), implementing the requested change, and ensuring consistency. For an access request, the DF must compile the required information (summary of data, processing activities, shared entities) in a clear, concise, and easily understandable format. The DPDP Rules are expected to prescribe specific timelines for responding to and fulfilling these requests, which DFs must strictly adhere to. Maintaining a detailed log of all requests, verification steps, and actions taken is essential for demonstrating compliance during potential audits.

## Managing Erasure Requests and Grievance Redressal

The right to erasure (part of Section 12) presents distinct challenges, particularly given India's complex regulatory landscape. While a Data Principal may request erasure, DFs must first assess if any legal obligations or legitimate business purposes necessitate continued retention. For instance, financial institutions regulated by the RBI or entities under SEBI norms often have strict data retention periods for transactional data, KYC information, or audit trails. Similarly, IRDAI regulations might mandate retention of policyholder data. In such cases, DFs must clearly communicate the reasons for partial or full rejection of an erasure request, citing the relevant legal provisions (e.g., Section 17(1) of DPDPA in conjunction with RBI Master Directions).

Grievance redressal (Section 13) requires a well-defined internal mechanism. Every Data Fiduciary must designate a Data Protection Officer (DPO) or a Grievance Officer, whose contact details are readily available. This officer is the first point of contact for Data Principals. The workflow should outline clear steps for acknowledging, investigating, and resolving grievances within stipulated timelines. If a Data Principal is dissatisfied with the resolution, the DPDPA allows them to appeal to the Data Protection Board of India.

## Sectoral Nuances and Global Comparisons

Indian companies operate under a multi-layered regulatory framework. While the DPDPA provides the overarching data protection law, sectoral regulators like RBI, SEBI, and IRDAI often impose specific data handling, retention, and security requirements. These sectoral norms, along with the IT Rules, 2021, must be harmonised with DPDPA obligations. For example, a bank cannot simply erase a customer's transaction history upon request if RBI guidelines mandate its retention for a specific period. The DPDPA acknowledges this balance, and DFs must navigate these overlapping requirements diligently.

Globally, the European Union's GDPR offers a comparable "right to be forgotten," but the DPDPA's approach, particularly with Section 17(1), provides a clear legal basis for DFs to balance individual rights with legal and regulatory obligations. This nuance is crucial for Indian businesses, preventing blanket erasure requests from undermining critical compliance requirements.

## Practical takeaway

Indian businesses, including their General Counsels and Data Protection Officers, must move beyond policy drafting to operationalising Data Principal rights. This means investing in dedicated privacy portals, automating identity verification processes, establishing clear internal data request routing and fulfilment procedures, and rigorously training staff across all departments. Crucially, DFs must map their data flows against DPDPA rights and relevant sectoral regulations to identify potential conflicts, especially concerning data retention. Proactive implementation of these workflows, coupled with meticulous record-keeping, will be key to demonstrating compliance and building trust with Data Principals in India's evolving digital economy.
