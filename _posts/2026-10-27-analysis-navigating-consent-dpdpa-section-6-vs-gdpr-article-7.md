As of October 27, 2026, India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) is firmly established, bringing a robust framework for data privacy that resonates with global standards while retaining unique Indian characteristics. A cornerstone of most modern privacy regimes, including the DPDPA and the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), is the concept of consent. Understanding the nuances of consent under DPDPA Section 6 compared to GDPR Article 7 is crucial for any organization operating in or with India. This analysis delves into the critical aspects of granularity, withdrawal, and the onus of proof, anchoring our discussion in the Indian legal landscape.

## Granularity and Specificity of Consent

Both the DPDPA and GDPR emphasize that consent must be specific and informed, rejecting broad, catch-all agreements. Under DPDPA Section 6(1)(a), consent must be "free, specific, informed, unconditional and unambiguous" and clearly indicate the Data Principal's agreement to the processing of their personal data for a "specified purpose." This requirement for a "specified purpose" implies a need for granularity, meaning data fiduciaries cannot seek blanket consent for multiple, disparate processing activities. Prior to seeking consent, Section 5 mandates that the Data Fiduciary must provide the Data Principal with an itemised notice describing the personal data to be collected and the purpose of processing.

Similarly, GDPR Article 7(2) stipulates that if consent is sought in the context of a written declaration which also concerns other matters, the request for consent must be presented in a manner that is clearly distinguishable from the other matters. Recital 32 of the GDPR further clarifies that consent should be given by a clear affirmative act, signifying a freely given, specific, informed, and unambiguous indication of the data subject's agreement. Both frameworks effectively disallow bundled consent, pushing organizations towards distinct consent requests for each specific processing operation. While the language is slightly different, the practical outcome is largely aligned: a high bar for specific, unbundled consent.

## Withdrawal of Consent

The right to withdraw consent is a fundamental aspect of data principal control in both jurisdictions. DPDPA Section 6(4) explicitly grants the Data Principal the right to withdraw consent at any time, with the crucial proviso that the ease of withdrawing consent must be "comparable to the ease with which consent was given." Upon withdrawal, the Data Fiduciary must cease processing the Data Principal's personal data, unless such processing is required or authorised by law.

This provision closely mirrors GDPR Article 7(3), which states that the data subject shall have the right to withdraw his or her consent at any time. It further mandates that "it shall be as easy to withdraw as to give consent." The withdrawal of consent does not affect the lawfulness of processing based on consent before its withdrawal. Both laws impose a significant operational burden on data fiduciaries to implement clear, accessible, and user-friendly mechanisms for consent withdrawal, ensuring that individuals can exercise this right without undue hurdles.

## Onus of Proof

Accountability is a core principle, and both the DPDPA and GDPR place the burden of demonstrating compliance squarely on the entity processing the data. DPDPA Section 6(5) unequivocally states that the "Data Fiduciary shall be able to demonstrate that a notice has been given and consent has been obtained in accordance with the provisions of this Act." This places a clear obligation on organizations to maintain robust records and audit trails of consent acquisition.

In parallel, GDPR Article 7(1) mandates that "where processing is based on consent, the controller shall be able to demonstrate that the data subject has consented to processing of his or her personal data." This identical requirement underscores a global consensus on accountability. For Indian businesses, this means meticulous record-keeping of consent notices, timestamps, and the specific purposes for which consent was granted is not merely good practice but a legal imperative.

## Broader Context: Indian Specifics and Regulatory Overlays

While the DPDPA provides the overarching framework, it is important to consider India's unique regulatory landscape, particularly the influence of sector-specific regulators. For instance, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has historically issued guidelines for financial institutions that often impose stricter or more granular consent requirements, especially concerning sensitive financial data and data localization. While the DPDPA sets a baseline, RBI's directives on digital lending or payment aggregators can introduce additional layers of consent protocols that are arguably *stricter* than the general DPDPA provisions. Similarly, elements of the Information Technology (Reasonable Security Practices and Procedures and Sensitive Personal Data or Information) Rules, 2011, though largely superseded by DPDPA for personal data, previously established a precedent for explicit consent for sensitive personal data, influencing the regulatory mindset.

In contrast, while GDPR also operates alongside sector-specific regulations (e.g., in health or financial services), the DPDPA's interaction with powerful sectoral regulators like the RBI can lead to a more complex and potentially *stricter* compliance environment for data fiduciaries in specific sectors within India. The DPDPA provides the foundational principles, but organizations must also navigate these additional regulatory overlays.

**Practical Takeaway**

For Indian businesses, General Counsels, and Data Protection Officers, the comparison reveals a strong alignment between the DPDPA and GDPR on the core tenets of consent. The emphasis on specific, informed, and unambiguous consent, the right to easy withdrawal, and the onus of proof resting on the Data Fiduciary are consistent across both regimes. This means that robust consent management platforms, clear and concise consent notices (as required by DPDPA Section 5), and streamlined withdrawal mechanisms are non-negotiable. Furthermore, Indian entities, especially those in regulated sectors like finance, must remember that the DPDPA sets the floor, not necessarily the ceiling. Sector-specific regulations, such as those from the RBI, may impose even stricter consent obligations, requiring a multi-layered compliance strategy. Meticulous record-keeping of consent processes is paramount to demonstrate compliance under DPDPA Section 6(5).
