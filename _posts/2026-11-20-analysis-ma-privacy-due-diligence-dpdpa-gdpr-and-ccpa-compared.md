As M&A activity continues to reshape global markets, the integration of data protection compliance into due diligence has become paramount. For Indian businesses, general counsels, and Data Protection Officers (DPOs), understanding the nuances of the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) in contrast to established frameworks like the GDPR and CCPA is crucial. This analysis anchors on the DPDPA, comparing its implications for M&A privacy due diligence against its foreign counterparts, highlighting areas of divergence and shared principles.

## Navigating Data Fiduciary Obligations Post-Acquisition

Under the DPDPA, an acquiring entity largely steps into the shoes of the target as a "Data Fiduciary." This means it inherits the responsibility to ensure all processing of personal data continues to be lawful. A central tenet of the DPDPA is the requirement for valid consent (Section 6) or a specified "legitimate use" (Section 7) for processing. In an M&A context, due diligence must rigorously assess whether the original basis for collecting and processing data by the target remains valid for the *new* purposes post-acquisition. If the acquiring entity intends to use the data for purposes not covered by the original consent or legitimate use, fresh consent may be required, or a new legitimate use must be established. This approach can be seen as stricter than the CCPA, which, under Section 1798.140(d)(2)(D), explicitly exempts the transfer of personal information as part of a merger or acquisition from being considered a "sale," provided the acquiring entity maintains control and continues to honor the consumer's rights and the original privacy policy.

The GDPR, similarly, requires a lawful basis for processing under Article 6. While "legitimate interests" is often invoked in M&A scenarios, the acquiring entity must still conduct a legitimate interests assessment and ensure transparency (Articles 13 and 14) regarding the change in control and new processing purposes. Unlike the CCPA's specific M&A exemption for "sale," neither the DPDPA nor the GDPR provide a direct carve-out for the transfer of personal data itself during an acquisition, instead focusing on the ongoing lawfulness of processing. This means DPDPA and GDPR due diligence must delve deeper into the *purpose* of data processing post-acquisition.

## Cross-Border Data Transfers: Divergent Approaches

Cross-border data transfers present a significant area of divergence. The GDPR, under Articles 44 to 50, imposes stringent requirements for transferring personal data outside the European Economic Area (EEA). Mechanisms like Standard Contractual Clauses (SCCs), Binding Corporate Rules (BCRs), or reliance on an adequacy decision are non-negotiable for GDPR-regulated entities. For an Indian company acquiring an EU-based target, or vice-versa, assessing and establishing these transfer mechanisms is a critical part of due diligence and post-acquisition integration. This makes GDPR compliance in M&A particularly complex for international deals.

The DPDPA, in contrast, takes a less prescriptive approach to cross-border transfers. Section 16 empowers the Central Government to notify "such countries or territories outside India to which a Data Fiduciary may transfer personal data." While this provision allows for potential future restrictions, as of November 2026, the DPDPA does not mandate specific transfer mechanisms akin to GDPR's SCCs or BCRs. The primary focus remains on ensuring the original lawful basis for processing (consent or legitimate use) extends to the cross-border transfer, and that the recipient adheres to the DPDPA's principles. This makes the DPDPA appear potentially looser on the *mechanics* of transfer than the GDPR, but the government's power to *restrict* transfers could introduce significant hurdles if specific countries are blacklisted.
