The Digital Personal Data Protection Act (DPDPA), 2023, now fully operational with the DPDP Rules, 2025, marks a significant shift in India's data governance. Among its most critical provisions is the mandate for personal data breach notification, fundamentally altering how organisations must respond to security incidents. This obligation, detailed in Section 10 of the DPDPA, demands a multi-faceted approach, balancing speed, transparency, and adherence to specific regulatory requirements.

## Understanding the DPDPA's Breach Notification Mandate

At its core, Section 10 of the DPDPA places a clear obligation on a Data Fiduciary (as defined in Section 2(10)) to notify both the Data Protection Board of India (the Board, established under Section 2(13)) and affected Data Principals (Section 2(11)) of any "personal data breach" (Section 2(21)). This signifies a move towards greater accountability and transparency, ensuring individuals are informed when their personal data is compromised. The DPDPA Rules, 2025, prescribed under Section 40, elaborate on the "form and manner" of these notifications, providing the granular details necessary for compliance. This includes defining what constitutes a reportable breach, which generally encompasses any unauthorised processing, accidental disclosure, alteration, or destruction of personal data that could lead to harm or inconvenience to a Data Principal.

## Navigating Timelines: The Multi-Layered Indian Approach

India's regulatory landscape for incident reporting is uniquely layered. While the DPDPA introduces new obligations, it does not supersede existing ones. The DPDP Rules, 2025 (e.g., Rule X, which is expected to specify timelines), mandate that Data Fiduciaries must notify the Data Protection Board of a personal data breach "without undue delay," often interpreted as within 72 hours of becoming aware of the breach, similar to global best practices like GDPR. However, the obligation to notify affected Data Principals may be subject to a risk assessment, with notification required "without undue delay" if the breach is likely to result in a high risk to their rights and freedoms.

Crucially, this DPDPA timeline operates alongside existing cybersecurity incident reporting requirements. Directions issued by CERT-In under Section 70B of the Information Technology Act, 2000, mandate reporting of certain cyber incidents within six hours of becoming aware of them. Furthermore, sectoral regulators like the Reserve Bank of India (RBI), Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI), and Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI) have their own stringent timelines. For instance, RBI's Master Directions often require banks and financial institutions to report cyber incidents within six hours to RBI and CERT-In. Data Fiduciaries must therefore develop a robust incident response plan that accommodates these cumulative and often tighter reporting windows, ensuring simultaneous or sequential reporting to multiple authorities.

## Content and Scope of Notification

The DPDP Rules, 2025 (e.g., Rule Y), are expected to prescribe the precise content required for breach notifications. For the Data Protection Board, this will likely include a detailed description of the nature of the personal data breach, the categories and approximate number of Data Principals and personal data records concerned, the likely consequences of the breach, and the measures taken or proposed to be taken by the Data Fiduciary to address the breach and mitigate its adverse effects. Contact details of the Data Protection Officer (DPO) or designated point of contact will also be essential.

For affected Data Principals, the notification content will be more focused on enabling them to take protective measures. This typically includes a clear explanation of the breach, the potential risks to them, advice on how to mitigate these risks (e.g., changing passwords, monitoring accounts), and contact information for further queries. Unlike the GDPR (Article 34), which generally requires notification to individuals only when the breach is "likely to result in a high risk," Section 10 of the DPDPA implies a broader obligation to notify "each affected Data Principal." However, the DPDP Rules, 2025, are anticipated to introduce a materiality or risk-based threshold for individual notification, preventing undue alarm for low-risk incidents while ensuring timely communication for serious breaches.

## Practicalities of Reporting to the Data Protection Board

Reporting to the Data Protection Board will likely be facilitated through a dedicated online portal or prescribed electronic format, as outlined in the DPDP Rules, 2025 (e.g., Rule Z). Data Fiduciaries must ensure their internal systems are capable of rapidly identifying, assessing, and documenting personal data breaches to meet these stringent reporting requirements. This includes having clear internal protocols for incident classification, impact assessment, and the collection of all necessary information for the notification forms. Furthermore, even if a breach does not meet the threshold for notification to Data Principals (if such a threshold is indeed introduced by the Rules), Data Fiduciaries are still expected to maintain comprehensive internal records of all personal data breaches, demonstrating their due diligence and accountability under the DPDPA.

**Practical Takeaway:**
Indian businesses, GCs, and DPOs must proactively review and update their incident response plans to align with the DPDPA and DPDP Rules, 2025. This involves establishing clear internal procedures for identifying, assessing, and responding to personal data breaches within the tight timelines mandated by the DPDPA, CERT-In, and sectoral regulators. Invest in robust technical and organisational measures, conduct regular breach simulation exercises, and ensure your DPO or designated privacy team is empowered to make rapid, informed decisions. Remember, compliance is not just about reporting; it's about demonstrating a comprehensive commitment to data protection and accountability.
