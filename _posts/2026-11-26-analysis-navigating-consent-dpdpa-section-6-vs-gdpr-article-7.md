As India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) firmly establishes its framework, understanding its core tenets, particularly around consent, becomes paramount for any entity processing personal data. The DPDPA's approach to consent, enshrined primarily in Section 6, shares foundational similarities with the European Union's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) Article 7, yet presents distinct nuances that merit close examination. For Indian businesses navigating both domestic regulations and global data flows, a comparative analysis of granularity, withdrawal mechanisms, and the onus of proof is critical.

## Granularity of Consent: A Tailored Approach

Both the DPDPA and GDPR emphasize that consent must be specific, informed, and unambiguous. Under DPDPA Section 6(1)(a), consent must be "free, specific, informed, unconditional and an unambiguous affirmation." This implies a clear requirement for granularity, meaning consent should be sought for each distinct purpose of processing. The "unconditional" aspect is particularly noteworthy; it suggests that consent cannot be bundled with other terms and conditions, nor can it be a prerequisite for accessing a service unless strictly necessary for that service.

GDPR Article 7(2) similarly mandates that if consent is given in the context of a written declaration concerning other matters, the request for consent must be presented in a manner "clearly distinguishable from the other matters." This provision aims to prevent 'take-it-or-leave-it' bundled consent. While DPDPA Section 6(1)(a) doesn't explicitly detail the presentation format, its "unconditional" requirement achieves a similar effect by preventing consent from being a hidden clause within broader agreements. In this regard, the DPDPA's "unconditional" stipulation can be seen as equally, if not more, stringent than GDPR's explicit presentational requirements, ensuring that consent is a standalone, deliberate choice.

## The Right to Withdraw Consent: Empowering Data Principals

A cornerstone of both regimes is the Data Principal's (or Data Subject's) unfettered right to withdraw consent. DPDPA Section 6(4) explicitly states that a Data Principal "shall have the right to withdraw his consent at any time." Crucially, it mandates that the withdrawal process must be "as easy as it is to give consent." Upon receiving a withdrawal, the Data Fiduciary must cease processing the personal data for the purpose for which consent was withdrawn, unless another legal basis for processing exists under the DPDPA.

GDPR Article 7(3) echoes this principle, affirming the data subject's right to withdraw consent at any time and stipulating that withdrawal must be "as easy as to give consent." It further clarifies that the lawfulness of processing based on consent before its withdrawal remains unaffected. Both frameworks align closely on this front, reflecting a shared commitment to empowering individuals with control over their data. The DPDPA's explicit mention of ceasing processing upon withdrawal, unless an alternative legal basis applies, provides clear operational guidance, making it equally robust in its protection.

## Onus of Proof: Demonstrating Compliance

The burden of proving valid consent rests squarely on the entity processing the data in both jurisdictions. DPDPA Section 6(5) unambiguously states that "the Data Fiduciary shall be able to demonstrate that the Data Principal gave consent in accordance with the provisions of this Act." This places a clear accountability requirement on businesses to maintain records and mechanisms that can evidence valid consent.

Similarly, GDPR Article 7(1) mandates that "where processing is based on consent, the controller shall be able to demonstrate that the data subject has consented to processing of his or her personal data." This strong alignment between the DPDPA and GDPR underscores a universal principle in modern data protection: consent is not merely a formality but a demonstrable legal basis for processing, requiring robust record-keeping and auditable processes. Indian law, therefore, is equally strict in requiring businesses to actively prove compliance, rather than assuming consent.

## Interplay with Indian Sectoral Regulations

While the DPDPA provides a comprehensive framework, businesses in India must also contend with existing sectoral regulations that may impose additional or more granular consent requirements. For instance, the Reserve Bank of India (RBI) has issued various guidelines, such as those pertaining to digital lending or credit information companies, which often include specific consent mandates for sharing financial data or accessing customer information. These RBI directives, predating the DPDPA, frequently demand explicit, granular consent for specific actions, sometimes even requiring physical signatures or multi-factor authentication for sensitive transactions.

DPDPA Section 40(2) clarifies that its provisions are "in addition to and not in derogation of" any other law for the time being in force. This means that where sectoral regulations, like those from the RBI, impose stricter or more specific consent obligations, businesses must adhere to both sets of rules. This layered regulatory environment can, in practice, make the Indian consent landscape more demanding for certain sectors compared to the GDPR, which, while comprehensive, does not typically have such a dense overlay of pre-existing, sector-specific consent requirements.

Practical takeaway: Indian businesses, GCs, and DPOs must implement consent mechanisms that are not only DPDPA-compliant but also satisfy any sector-specific requirements, particularly in regulated industries like finance. This involves designing user interfaces that clearly distinguish consent requests for each purpose, ensuring withdrawal is frictionless, and maintaining meticulous records of consent acquisition and withdrawal. A 'consent dashboard' for Data Principals to manage their preferences could be a valuable tool. Remember that merely obtaining consent is insufficient; being able to *demonstrate* its validity and proper management is key to avoiding penalties.
