The landscape of data breach notification has become a critical compliance area for organisations worldwide, and India, with its Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023 (DPDPA) and accompanying DPDP Rules now in effect, stands at a pivotal juncture. For Indian businesses navigating this complex terrain, understanding domestic requirements in comparison to global benchmarks like the EU's GDPR, Singapore's PDPA, and Australia's NDB scheme is essential. This analysis anchors on India's multi-faceted approach to breach notification, comparing its timelines and triggers against these key international frameworks.

## Timelines for Notifying Regulatory Authorities

India's approach to notifying regulatory bodies presents a layered complexity. While the DPDPA, 2023, under Section 18(1), mandates Data Fiduciaries to notify the Data Protection Board of India (Board) of a personal data breach, the specific timelines are prescribed in the DPDP Rules. These rules, now operational, generally stipulate that the Board must be notified "without undue delay and, where feasible, not later than 72 hours after becoming aware" of a personal data breach. This aligns closely with the EU's General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) Article 33(1), which similarly requires notification to the supervisory authority within 72 hours.

However, India also maintains sector-specific and general cyber incident reporting obligations that are significantly stricter. The Reserve Bank of India's (RBI) Master Direction on IT Governance, Risk, Controls, and Operations mandates regulated entities to report cyber incidents to the RBI within 6 hours of detection. Similarly, the Indian Computer Emergency Response Team (CERT-In) Directions, issued under Section 70B of the Information Technology Act, 2000, require all service providers, data centres, body corporates, and government organisations to report cyber incidents to CERT-In within 6 hours. These 6-hour windows are considerably more stringent than the DPDPA Rules and the GDPR's 72-hour benchmark, placing India's regulated entities and critical infrastructure providers under some of the tightest reporting deadlines globally.

In contrast, Singapore's Personal Data Protection Act (PDPA) Section 26C requires notification to the Personal Data Protection Commission (PDPC) "as soon as practicable" if the breach is likely to result in significant harm or is of a significant scale. Australia's Notifiable Data Breaches (NDB) scheme, under Part IIIC of the Privacy Act 1988, similarly requires notification to the Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC) "as soon as practicable" after an entity becomes aware of reasonable grounds to believe an eligible data breach has occurred. These "as soon as practicable" standards offer more flexibility than fixed hour counts, but still demand prompt action based on the circumstances.

## Communicating with Affected Data Principals

The obligation to inform affected individuals, or 'Data Principals' as per the DPDPA, also varies. India's DPDP Rules, in line with global best practices, generally require Data Fiduciaries to notify affected Data Principals "without undue delay" if the personal data breach is likely to result in a "high risk" to their rights and freedoms. This mirrors GDPR Article 34(1), which mandates communication to data subjects "without undue delay" when the breach is "likely to result in a high risk" to their rights and freedoms. Both frameworks allow for exceptions, such as when measures have been implemented to mitigate the high risk.

Singapore's PDPA Section 26D also requires notification to affected individuals "as soon as practicable" if the breach is likely to result in "significant harm" to them. Australia's NDB scheme, under Section 26WK, similarly requires notification to individuals "as soon as practicable" if an "eligible data breach" (one likely to result in serious harm) has occurred. While the language differs ("high risk" vs. "significant harm" vs. "serious harm"), the underlying principle across these jurisdictions is to inform individuals promptly when the breach poses a substantial threat to them, enabling them to take protective measures. India's DPDP Rules align closely with the GDPR's prescriptive approach here.

## Defining the "Breach" and Risk Thresholds

The trigger for notification often hinges on the definition of a breach and the associated risk threshold. The DPDPA defines a "personal data breach" broadly in Section 2(1)(p) as any unauthorised processing of personal data, including its accidental disclosure, acquisition, sharing, use, alteration, destruction, or loss. The DPDP Rules further elaborate on the types of breaches and the assessment criteria for determining "high risk" to Data Principals.

GDPR Article 4(12) defines a "personal data breach" as a breach of security leading to the accidental or unlawful destruction, loss, alteration, unauthorised disclosure of, or access to, personal data. It then applies risk thresholds ("likely to result in a risk" for authorities, "high risk" for data subjects) to determine notification obligations. Singapore's PDPA and Australia's NDB scheme both introduce specific thresholds of "significant harm" or "serious harm" to individuals as prerequisites for mandatory notification, rather than requiring notification for every breach. India's DPDP Rules, by incorporating a "high risk" threshold for Data Principals, align with the GDPR's nuanced approach, requiring a risk assessment before individual notification. However, the broader "cyber incident" reporting under CERT-In and RBI does not necessarily require a personal data element or a high-risk threshold, making their scope wider and triggers more immediate.

## Practical Takeaway

For Indian businesses, particularly those operating across sectors or internationally, the breach notification landscape demands a sophisticated, multi-pronged strategy. The DPDPA Rules provide a clear framework for personal data breaches, largely mirroring GDPR's 72-hour and "without undue delay" requirements. However, the concurrent and often stricter 6-hour reporting mandates from CERT-In and RBI for cyber incidents mean that many organisations face significantly tighter deadlines than their global counterparts. Businesses must establish robust incident response plans that can differentiate between a general cyber incident and a personal data breach, assess risk to Data Principals swiftly, and cater to the varying timelines and reporting channels. Prioritising the strictest applicable timeline (often 6 hours for regulated entities or cyber incidents) is crucial, ensuring compliance across all relevant Indian laws while also preparing for the DPDPA's specific personal data breach notification requirements.
